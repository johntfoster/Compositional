\section{Material form of conservation of mass}
Given a differential mass ${\rm d}m^\al_\xi$ of component $\al$ in phase $\xi$, define the component partial density per current mixture volume by
%
\[
  \rho^\al_\xi(\mbf{x}, t) = \frac{{\rm d}m^\al_\xi}{\dv},
\]
%
where $\dv = {\rm d}x_1 {\rm d}x_2 {\rm d}x_3$ is a current-configuration volume element of the mixture.  The current configuration is a common mixture volume occupied by all components simultaneously.  Each phase $\xi$ motion is tracked from its own reference coordinates $\mbf{X}_\xi$ and reference volume element
%
\[
  \dVxi = ({\rm d}X_1)_\xi({\rm d}X_2)_\xi({\rm d}X_3)_\xi.
\]
%
The phase reference coordinates are related to the common current coordinates by a one-to-one, invertible, and continuously differentiable map on the region where that phase is present,
%
\[
  \mbf{x} = \bs{\chi}_\xi(\mbf{X}_\xi,t),
\]
%
with phase velocity $\mbf{v}_\xi = \partial \bs{\chi}_\xi/\partial t$ and Jacobian
%
\[
  J_\xi = \det\left(\p{\bs{\chi}_\xi}{\mbf{X}_\xi}\right).
\]
%
Let $\rho_{\xi0}^\al(\mbf{X}_\xi)$ denote the initial (evaluted at $\mbf{x}(t=0) = \mbf{X}_\xi$) component partial density, and let $\mathcal{C}^\al_\xi(\mbf{X}_\xi,t)$ denote the mass of component $\alpha$ added to phase $\xi$ per unit phase reference volume by any internal conversion mechanism.  These mechanisms include both chemical reactions and pure phase transformations.  Conservation of mass for component $\al$ in phase $\xi$ over an arbitrary material region can then be written as
%
\begin{align}
  \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi 0}} \left(\rho_{\xi 0}^\al + \mc{C}^\al_\xi\right)\dVxi
  &= \int_{\mc{B}} \rho_\xi^\al {\rm d}v 
  = \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi 0}} \rho_{\xi}^\al J_\xi \dVxi .
  \label{eq:material_mass}
\end{align}
%
Here $\mc{B}$ is the common current mixture region corresponding, through the phase-$\xi$ map, to the phase reference region $\mc{B}_{\xi0}$.  Since \eqref{eq:material_mass} must hold for arbitrary $\mc{B}_{\xi0}$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\xi 0}^\alpha + \mc{C}^\alpha_\xi = \rho_\xi^\alpha J_\xi.
  \label{eq:material_mass_2}
\end{equation}
%
The material time derivative of a quantity $\square_\xi$ following the velocity of phase $\zeta$ is
%
\[
  \frac{D_\zeta \square_\xi}{Dt}
  = \frac{\partial \square_\xi}{\partial t}
  + \mbf{v}_\zeta \cdot\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\square_\xi,
\]
%
and we use the shorthand $\dot\square_\xi = D_\xi \square_\xi/Dt$ for the material time derivative following phase $\xi$.  Taking the material time derivative of \eqref{eq:material_mass_2} following phase $\xi$ and using $\dot{J}_\xi = J_\xi \nabla_\mbf{x}\cdot\mbf{v}_\xi$ gives
%
\begin{align}
  \frac{D_\xi \rho_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
  + \rho_\xi^\alpha \nabla_\mbf{x}\cdot\mbf{v}_\xi
  &= \frac{\dot{\mc {C}}^\alpha_\xi}{J_\xi} .
  \label{eq:component_material_mass}
\end{align}
%
We define the current production rate per unit current mixture volume by
%
\[
  \dot{c}^\al_\xi(\mbf{x},t) = \frac{\dot{\mc{C}}^\al_\xi}{J_\xi}.
\]
%
Expanding \eqref{eq:component_material_mass} gives the spatial component mass balance of the $\xi$ phase
%
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial \rho_\xi^\alpha}{\partial t}
  + \nabla_\mbf{x}\cdot\left(\rho_\xi^\alpha\mbf{v}_\xi\right)
  = \dot{c}^\alpha_\xi .
  \label{eq:component_spatial_mass}
\end{align}
%
Now introduce the phase partial density $\rho_\xi = \sum_\alpha \rho_\xi^\alpha$ and the mass fraction of component $\alpha$ in phase $\xi$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \eta^\alpha_\xi = \frac{\rho^\alpha_\xi}{\rho_\xi},
  \qquad \sum_\alpha \eta_\xi^\alpha = 1.
  \label{eq:phase_mass_fraction}
\end{equation}
%
The phase volume fraction is defined by $\phi_\xi = \dv_\xi/\dv$, and the intrinsic density by $\bar\rho_\xi = {\rm d}m_\xi/\dv_\xi$, so that
%
\[
  \rho_\xi = \phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi,
  \qquad
  \rho_\xi^\alpha = \phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi \eta^\alpha_\xi.
\]
%
Substituting this representation into \eqref{eq:component_spatial_mass} gives
%
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi \eta^\al_\xi\right)
  + \nabla_\mbf{x}\cdot\left(\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi \eta^\al_\xi \mbf{v}_\xi\right)
  = \dot{c}^\alpha_\xi .
  \label{eq:spatial_mass_2}
\end{align}
%
All internal conversion mechanisms are parameterized by current progress rates $\dot r_m$ and phase/component stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{c}_\xi^\alpha = \sum_m \nu_{\xi m}^\alpha \dot{r}_m .
  \label{eq:unified_stoich_current}
\end{equation}
The index $m$ includes chemical reactions and pure phase transformations.  A pure phase transformation of component $\alpha$ from phase $\xi$ to phase $\zeta$ is the special one-to-one stoichiometry $\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=-1$, $\nu_{\zeta m}^\alpha=+1$, with all other entries zero.  A chemical reaction may change both component identity and phase label.  If each mechanism conserves total mass, $\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=0$, then the overall mixture mass is conserved automatically.  Component conservation is recovered for those pure phase-transformation mechanisms whose stoichiometry has zero sum over phases for the fixed component.

\section{Variational Derivation}
\label{sec:variational}

We derive the governing equations from Hamilton's extended principle, following Bedford \& Drumheller~\cite{drumheller1980thermomechanical}, adapted to a compositional mixture system containing phases (solid, aquous, oliec, and gas phases) and arbitrary miscible components $\alpha$ within each phase.

Each phase has volume fraction~$\phi_\xi$, intrinsic density~$\bar\rho_\xi$, bulk density $\rho_\xi = \phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi$, velocity~$\mbf{v}_\xi$, deformation gradient~$\mbf{F}_\xi$, and Jacobian $J_\xi = \det(\mbf{F}_\xi)$.  

\subsection{Variational principle and its terms}

The extended Hamilton's principle is
\begin{equation*}
  \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left[\delta(T - U) + \delta W + \sum_k \delta C_k\right] dt = 0,
\end{equation*}
with kinetic energy, internal energy, virtual work, and four constraints:
\begin{align}
T &= \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}}\frac{1}{2} \rho_\xi \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \dv,
  \label{eq:T_def} \\
  U &= \sum_{\xi}\int_{\mc{B}} \rho_{\xi}\,\psi_{\xi}(\mbf{F}_{\xi}, \bar\rho_{\xi}, \eta^\al_{\xi})\dv
    + \int_{\mc{B}} \phi\,\gamma(S_a, S_g) \dv, 
  \label{eq:U_def} \\
  \delta W &= \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} (\rho_\xi\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_\xi)\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi \,\dv
  - \sum_m\int_{\mc B}\mc A_m\,\delta\dot r_m\,\dv,
  \label{eq:W_def} \\
  C_1 &= \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1\right) \,\dv,
  \label{eq:C1_def} \\
  C_2 &= \sum_\xi \sum_\al \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi 0}} \tau^\al_\xi \left( J_\xi - \frac{\phi_{\xi 0} \eta^\al_{\xi 0} \bar\rho_{\xi 0} + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \eta^\al_\xi \bar\rho_\xi} \right) \dV_\xi, 
  \label{eq:C2_def} \\
  C_3 &= \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \pi_\xi \left( \sum_\alpha \eta_\xi^\alpha - 1 \right) \dv,
  \label{eq:C3_def} \\
  C_4 &= \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc B}\mu_\xi^\alpha\left(\frac{\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}-\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m\right)\dv .
  \label{eq:C4_def}
\end{align}
%
The affinities $\mc A_m$ are introduced as generalized conversion forces in the
virtual-work one-form, conjugate to the current progress-rate variations
$\delta\dot r_m$.  The kinematic constraint $C_4$ ties those progress rates to
the phase-attached accumulated sources through
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.  This distinction is essential:
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ is not a freely varied Eulerian scalar, because variation of
the phase map also varies $J_\xi$.  Chemical reactions and pure phase
transformations are both represented by the unified stoichiometric
parameterization \eqref{eq:unified_stoich_current}.  We treat
$\mbf{x}_\xi$, $\eta^\alpha_\xi$, $\phi_\xi$, $\bar\rho_\xi$, $\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha$,
$\dot r_m$, $\mu_\xi^\alpha$, and $\mc A_m$ as variational variables, with
additional constitutive closure required for kinetically limited mechanisms.

\subsection{Extending Bedford and Drumheller identity 1.23}
\label{sec:extended_bd_identity}

The accumulated source $\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha$ is attached to the phase-$\xi$
reference coordinates, while its current production rate is
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.  Thus variations of source
terms must be pushed forward with the same phase map as the corresponding
component density.
%
For any phase field $\Gamma_\xi$, the Bedford--Drumheller identity extends componentwise as
\begin{align}
  \delta \int_{\mc{B}} \rho^\alpha_\xi \Gamma_\xi \, \dv
  &= \int_{\mc{B}} \left( \rho^\alpha_\xi\delta\Gamma_\xi
  + \frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}\Gamma_\xi\right)\dv .
  \label{eq:bd128_mod2}
\end{align}
summing both sides of \eqref{eq:bd128_mod2} over $\alpha$ gives
\begin{align}
  \delta \int_{\mc{B}} \rho_\xi \psi_\xi \, \dv
  &= \int_{\mc{B}} \left(\rho_\xi \delta \Gamma_\xi + \sum_\alpha \frac{\delta \mc{C}^\al_\xi}{J_\xi} \Gamma_\xi  \right)  \dv
  \label{eq:bd128_mod}
\end{align}
%
where we've used $\rho_\xi = \sum_\alpha \rho^\alpha_\xi$.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Variation of the kinetic energy}
\label{sec:var_T_app}
% ============================================================

The variation of kinetic energy using \eqref{eq:bd128_mod} is then
\begin{equation}
  \delta T = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \rho_\xi\,\mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \delta\mbf{v}_\xi + \frac{1}{2} \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \sum_\al \frac{\delta \mc{C}^\al_\xi}{J_\xi} \dv.
\end{equation}
Since $\delta\mbf{v}_\xi = \mathrm{d}(\delta\mbf{x}_\xi)/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi\vert_{t_1} = \delta\mbf{x}_\xi\vert_{t_2} = \mbf{0}$, integration by parts in time gives
\begin{equation}
  \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \delta T \dt
  = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \left(-\rho_\xi\,\mbf{a}_\xi - \sum_\al \dot c^\alpha_\xi \mbf{v}_\xi \right) \cdot \delta\mbf{x}_\xi + \frac{1}{2} \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \sum_\al \frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}  \dv \dt
  \label{eq:deltaT_app}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Variation of the phase free energies}
\label{sec:var_Uxi}
% ============================================================

For every phase, solid or mobile, let $\psi_\xi$ denote the specific Helmholtz
free energy.  The corresponding contribution to the total free energy is written
in the same current density-weighted form,
\begin{align}
  U_\xi &= \int_{\mc B}\rho_\xi\psi_\xi(\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^1, \eta_\xi^2, \dots, \eta_\xi^N)\,\dv \notag \\
        &= \int_{\mc B}\rho_\xi\psi_\xi(\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta)\,\dv
  \label{eq:U_xi_uniform}
\end{align}
Here $\eta_\xi^\beta$ denotes the full list of component mass fractions
$\{\eta_\xi^1,\ldots,\eta_\xi^N\}$; the index $\beta$ is not summed in this
argument list.
The constitutive dependence on $\mbf F_\xi$ may be trivial for mobile phases,
but keeping it in the notation allows one calculation for all phases.  The local
free-energy variation is
\begin{align}
  \delta\psi_\xi
  &= \left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\mbf F_\xi}\right\vert_{\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}:\delta\mbf F_\xi
   + \left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\bar\rho_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\delta\bar\rho_\xi
   + \sum_\alpha
  \left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^{\beta\neq\alpha}}
  \delta\eta_\xi^\alpha .
  \label{eq:delta_psi_uniform}
\end{align}
Using \eqref{eq:bd128_mod}, integrating the $\delta\mbf F_\xi$ term by parts,
and collecting independent variations gives
\begin{align}
  \delta U_\xi
  &= \sum_\xi\int_{\mc B}\Bigg[
    -\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma'_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf x_\xi
    + \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\bar\rho_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\delta\bar\rho_\xi \notag\\
  &\qquad
    + \sum_\alpha \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^{\beta\neq\alpha}}\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha
    + \sum_\alpha \psi_\xi\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
  \Bigg]\dv + \text{boundary terms}.
  \label{eq:deltaU_uniform}
\end{align}
The corresponding Cauchy stress is
\begin{align}
  \bs\sigma'_\xi
  \equiv \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\mbf F_\xi}\right\vert_{\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\mbf F_\xi^T .
  \label{eq:var_cauchy_def}
\end{align}
For mobile phases this term vanishes when $\psi_\xi$ has no dependence on
$\mbf F_\xi$.  For a solid pulled back to its reference configuration for
computation, one may introduce $W_{\xi_s}=J_{\xi_s}\rho_{\xi_s}\psi_{\xi_s}$ and
$\mbf P_{\xi_s}=J_{\xi_s}\bs\sigma'_{\xi_s}\mbf F_{\xi_s}^{-T}
=\partial W_{\xi_s}/\partial\mbf F_{\xi_s}$ for the corresponding classical
reference energy density.



% ============================================================
\subsection{Variation of the interfacial energies}
\label{sec:var_gamma}
% ============================================================

With three mobile phases (aqueous, oleic, and gas), let
\[
  \phi = \phi_a + \phi_o + \phi_g,
  \qquad
  S_a = \frac{\phi_a}{\phi},
  \qquad
  S_g = \frac{\phi_g}{\phi},
\]
so that $S_o = 1-S_a-S_g = \phi_o/\phi$.  The saturations are used here only as
ratios of the primitive volume-fraction fields; the variations are still taken
with respect to $\phi_a$, $\phi_o$, and $\phi_g$.  We specialize the interfacial energy density to
\[
  U_\gamma = \int_{\mc{B}} \phi\,\gamma\!\left(S_a,S_g\right)\,\dv.
\]
Following the saturation-based capillary-pressure construction used for the
two-mobile-phase case, but now taking oil as the reference mobile phase, define
\begin{equation}
  p_{c,oa} \equiv -\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial S_a},
  \qquad
  p_{c,og} \equiv -\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial S_g} .
  \label{eq:pc_def}
\end{equation}
Here $p_{c,oa}=\bar p_o-\bar p_a$ is the oil--aqueous capillary pressure and
$p_{c,og}=\bar p_o-\bar p_g$ is the additional oil--gas capillary pressure.
Using $\delta\phi=\delta\phi_a+\delta\phi_o+\delta\phi_g$ and
\[
  \delta S_a=\frac{\delta\phi_a-S_a\delta\phi}{\phi},
  \qquad
  \delta S_g=\frac{\delta\phi_g-S_g\delta\phi}{\phi},
\]
the algebraic part of the interfacial variation can be written as
\begin{align}
  \delta\!\left(\phi\gamma\right)
  &= \gamma\,\delta\phi
     + \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial S_a}\left(\delta\phi_a-S_a\delta\phi\right)
     + \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial S_g}\left(\delta\phi_g-S_g\delta\phi\right) \notag\\
  &= \gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g,
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  \gamma_o &= \gamma + S_a p_{c,oa}+S_g p_{c,og}, \notag\\
  \gamma_a &= \gamma_o-p_{c,oa}, \notag\\
  \gamma_g &= \gamma_o-p_{c,og}.
  \label{eq:primitive_gamma_derivatives}
\end{align}
Consequently the capillary pressures are the oil-referenced differences of the
primitive interfacial potentials,
\begin{equation}
  p_{c,oa} \equiv \bar p_o-\bar p_a \equiv \gamma_o-\gamma_a,
  \qquad
  p_{c,og} \equiv \bar p_o-\bar p_g \equiv \gamma_o-\gamma_g .
\end{equation}

The spatial fields must be varied using the inter-species transport identity.
For the phase displacement $\delta\mbf x_\xi$, the volume-fraction variation
contains the transported part
\[
  \delta_x\phi_\xi = -\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot(\phi_\xi\delta\mbf x_\xi),
\]
in addition to the independent coefficient variation used in the algebraic
Euler--Lagrange equations.  Consequently,
\begin{align}
  \delta U_\gamma
  &= \int_{\mc B}\left(\gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g\right)\dv \notag \\
  &\quad
  + \int_{\mc B}\left[\phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a\cdot\delta\mbf x_a
      + \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_o\cdot\delta\mbf x_o
      + \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_g\cdot\delta\mbf x_g\right]\dv
  + \text{boundary terms}.
  \label{eq:deltaU_fg}
\end{align}
This form keeps the gradient terms generated by the spatial dependence of the
volume fractions.  They cancel against the corresponding multiplier gradients in
the phase momentum balances after the volume-fraction Euler--Lagrange equations
are used.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_1$: volume-fraction}
\label{sec:var_C1_app}
% ============================================================

The constraint $C_1 = \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1)\,\dv$ is written in the current configuration, so the natural variation is Eulerian, i.e. taken at fixed spatial point $\mbf{x}$.  Since each $\phi_\xi$ is a constituent-attached spatial field, we use the Bedford--Drumheller identity
\begin{equation}
  \left.\delta\phi_\xi\right\vert_{\mbf{x}}
  = \delta\phi_\xi - \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi,
  \label{eq:delta_phi_BD_local}
\end{equation}
to express the variation in terms of the spatial variation $\delta\phi_\xi$ and the motion variation $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$. Therefore
\begin{align}
  \delta C_1
  &= \int_{\mc{B}} \delta\lambda \left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1\right) \dv
  + \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda\left(\delta\phi_\xi - \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi\right) \dv.
  \label{eq:deltaC1_app}
\end{align}
The first term enforces the saturation constraint $\sum_\xi\phi_\xi = 1$. The remaining terms contribute $\lambda$ to each $\delta\phi_\xi$ equation and $-\lambda\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi$ to each momentum variation $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_2$: component material mass balance}
\label{sec:var_C2_app}
% ============================================================

The component material mass balance constraint is
\begin{equation}
  C_2 = \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}\tau_\xi^\alpha \left(J_\xi - \frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0} \eta^\al_{\xi 0} + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha} \right) \,dV_\xi.
  \label{eq:C2_component_measure}
\end{equation}
%
%
Hence
%
\begin{align}
  \delta C_2
  &= \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
      \tau_\xi^\alpha \delta J_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
  \notag \\
  &\quad
  + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
      \tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi^2\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha}\,\delta\phi_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
  \notag \\
  &\quad
  + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
      \tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi^2 \eta^\al_\xi}\,\delta\bar\rho_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
  \notag \\
  &\quad
  + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
      \tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi (\eta^\al_\xi)^2}\,\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha \, {\rm d}V_\xi
  \notag \\
  &\quad
  - \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
      \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha}\,\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha \, {\rm d}V_\xi.
  \label{eq:deltaC2_lines}
\end{align}
Using $\dv = J_\xi {\rm d}V_\xi$ and the constraint $\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha+\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha=J_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha$, the individual contributions push forward as follows.

\paragraph{$\delta J_\xi$ terms (from $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$):}
\begin{align}
  \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
    \tau_\xi^\alpha \delta J_\xi \, dV_\xi
  &= -\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}}
    \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\tau_\xi^\alpha \cdot \delta\mbf{x}_\xi \, \dv
    + \text{b.t.}
  \label{eq:C2_dJ}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Spatial field terms:}
\begin{align}
  \delta C_2 \supset \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc{B}}
  \left[
    \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi}\delta\phi_\xi
    + \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\bar\rho_\xi}\delta\bar\rho_\xi
    + \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha
    - \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha}\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
  \right]\dv .
  \label{eq:C2_spatial_push}
\end{align}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_3$: mass partition within phase}
\label{sec:var_C3_app}
% ============================================================

\begin{align}
  \delta C_3
  &= \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \delta\pi_\xi \left(\sum_\al \eta_\xi^\al - 1\right) \dv
  + \sum_\xi \sum_\al \int_{\mc{B}} \pi_\xi \left(\delta\eta^\al_\xi - \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\eta_\xi^\al\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi\right) \dv, \notag \\
  &= \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \delta\pi_\xi \left(\sum_\al \eta_\xi^\al - 1\right) \dv
  + \sum_\xi \sum_\al \int_{\mc{B}} \pi_\xi \delta\eta^\al_\xi \dv.
  \label{eq:deltaC3_app}
\end{align}
%
The first line vanishes on the constraint surface $\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha = 1$ and recovers it as an Euler--Lagrange equation.  The second line contributes $\pi_\xi$ to each $\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha$ Euler--Lagrange equation.

\subsection{Stoichiometric conversion variations}
\label{sec:stoich_conversion_variations}
% ============================================================
Conversion sources are restricted by the current-rate kinematic constraint
\eqref{eq:C4_def}.  Define the component conversion potential coefficient
\begin{equation}
  G_\xi^\alpha
  \equiv
  \frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
  -\psi_\xi
  -\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha} .
  \label{eq:conversion_component_potential}
\end{equation}
The variation of $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$ must be taken before
using the stoichiometric projection, because the denominator depends on the phase
map.  Repeating the transport calculation used for phase-attached rates gives
\begin{align}
  \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta C_4\,\dt
  &=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
  \delta\mu_\xi^\alpha\left(\dot c_\xi^\alpha-
  \sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m\right)\dv\dt \notag\\
  &\quad+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
  \left[-\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
  +\dot c_\xi^\alpha\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha\cdot\delta\mbf x_\xi\right]
  \dv\dt \notag\\
  &\quad-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_m
  \left(\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)
  \delta\dot r_m\,\dv\dt+\text{b.t.}
  \label{eq:C4_variation}
\end{align}
The affinity is not introduced by allowing $\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ to vary freely.
It is the generalized conversion force in the virtual-work form
\begin{equation}
  \delta W_{\rm conv}=-\sum_m\int_{\mc B}\mc A_m\delta\dot r_m\,\dv .
  \label{eq:conversion_virtual_work_affinity}
\end{equation}
Consequently variation with respect to $\dot r_m$ gives the projected relation
\begin{equation}
  \mc A_m + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=0,
  \qquad m=1,\ldots,N_m.
  \label{eq:affinity_multiplier_projection}
\end{equation}
Thus $\mu_\xi^\alpha$ is a kinematic multiplier enforcing the phase-attached
push-forward and stoichiometry, while $\mc A_m$ is the thermodynamic force
conjugate to mechanism progress.  The term
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha\nabla\mu_\xi^\alpha$ is the part of the mechanical coupling
that would be lost if $\delta\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ were treated as a free Eulerian
variation.

\subsection{Collected variational principle}
\label{sec:collected_var}
% ============================================================

Evaluating all variations and collecting by independent variations $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$, $\delta\phi_\xi$, $\delta\bar\rho_\xi$, $\delta\eta^\alpha_\xi$, and $\delta\dot r_m$ gives
\begin{align}
  0 = & \sum_{\xi} \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
    -\rho_{\xi}\mbf{a}_{\xi}
    + \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\cdot\bs\sigma'_{\xi} + \rho_{\xi}\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_{\xi}
    - \lambda\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_{\xi}
    - \sum_\alpha\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\tau_{\xi}^\alpha
    + \sum_\alpha \dot c_{\xi}^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf{v}_{\xi}\right)
  \Bigg]\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_{\xi} \,\dv \notag \\
  &- \int_{\mc B}\left[
    \phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a\cdot\delta\mbf x_a
    + \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_o\cdot\delta\mbf x_o
    + \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_g\cdot\delta\mbf x_g
  \right]\dv \notag \\
  &+ \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
    \lambda + \sum_\alpha \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\phi_{\xi}}
  \Bigg] \delta\phi_{\xi} \,\dv \notag \\
  &- \int_{\mc B}\left(\gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g\right)\dv \notag \\
  &+ \sum_{\xi}\int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
    -\rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}\right\vert_{\mbf{F}_{\xi},\eta_\xi^\beta}
    + \sum_\alpha\frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}
  \Bigg] \delta\bar\rho_{\xi} \,\dv \notag \\
  &+ \sum_{\xi}\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
    \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha} + \pi_{\xi}
    - \rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta^\alpha_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta^{\beta\neq\alpha}_\xi}
  \Bigg] \delta\eta^\alpha_\xi \,\dv \notag \\
  &+ \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc B}\left[
    \tfrac12\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi-\psi_\xi
    -\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
    -\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha}
  \right]\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}\,\dv \notag\\
  &- \sum_m\int_{\mc B}\left(\mc A_m+\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)\delta\dot r_m\,\dv + \text{boundary terms}.
  \label{eq:collected_full}
\end{align}
The multiplier variations are omitted here because they return the constraint equations~\eqref{eq:C1_def}--\eqref{eq:C4_def}. Since all variations are considered arbitrary, each bracketed integrand must vanish pointwise, yielding the Euler--Lagrange equations.

\subsection{Euler--Lagrange equations}

The Euler--Lagrange equations generated by arbitrary $\delta\phi_\xi$ give,
for phases without an explicit capillary contribution,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_\xi \lambda = - \sum_\alpha \tau^\al_\xi .
  \label{eq:el_lambda}
\end{equation}
For the mobile phases the corresponding equations contain the primitive
capillary potentials discussed below.

The displacement equation obtained from the uniform part of the collected
principle is
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\xi}\mbf{a}_{\xi} =
    \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\cdot\bs\sigma'_{\xi} + \rho_{\xi}\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_{\xi}
    + \phi_{\xi} \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\lambda
    + \sum_\alpha \dot c_{\xi}^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf{v}_{\xi}\right),
  \label{eq:el_momentum_uniform}
\end{equation}
with the mobile-phase capillary-force additions displayed separately below.

The Euler--Lagrange equation associated with arbitrary $\delta\bar\rho_\xi$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\al \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\al}{\phi_{\xi}}
  = \bar\rho^2_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}
  \equiv \bar{p}_{\xi} .
  \label{eq:phase_pressure_all}
\end{equation}
The Euler--Lagrange equations associated with arbitrary $\delta\eta_{\xi}^\alpha$ give
\begin{equation}
  0 = \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha} + \pi_{\xi}
  - \rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\beta\neq\alpha}},
  \qquad \alpha=1,\ldots,N,
  \label{eq:el_eta_all}
\end{equation}
while variation with respect to $\pi_{\xi}$ returns the composition normalization
constraint
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\alpha=1}^N \eta_{\xi}^\alpha = 1.
  \label{eq:el_eta_constraint}
\end{equation}
Thus $\pi_{\xi}$ is the phase-level multiplier that enforces the redundant
composition coordinate.  Equation~\eqref{eq:el_eta_all} may be read
componentwise as
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\beta\neq\alpha}}
  - \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}
  = \pi_{\xi},
  \label{eq:pi_componentwise}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
  \rho_{\xi}\left(
    \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq\alpha}}
    - \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\beta}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq\beta}}
  \right)
  &= \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}
   - \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\beta}{\eta_{\xi}^\beta}.
  \label{eq:pi_difference}
\end{align}
Here $\kappa$ denotes the collection of composition components held fixed in the
partial derivative; it is not summed.

Choosing $\alpha=1$ and comparing against $j=2,3,\ldots,N$ gives a system of
$N-1$ equations,
%
\begin{align}
  \rho_{\xi}\left(
    \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^1}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq 1}}
    - \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^j}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq j}}
  \right)
  &= \frac{\tau_{\xi}^1}{\eta_{\xi}^1}
   - \frac{\tau_{\xi}^j}{\eta_{\xi}^j},
  \qquad j=2,\ldots,N.
  \label{eq:pi_difference_system}
\end{align}
The equations associated with the conversion variables are
\begin{align}
  \frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
  &= \frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
  -\psi_\xi
  -\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha},
  \label{eq:el_conversion_component}\\
  \mc A_m + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha &=0,
  \qquad m=1,\ldots,N_m.
  \label{eq:el_conversion_affinity}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:el_conversion_affinity} is not an additional closure relation
for the state variables.  It defines the mechanism affinity as the stoichiometric
projection of the phase-attached conversion potentials, but only after the
kinematic push-forward $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$ has been
varied.  The closure enters only when one prescribes either an equilibrium
condition $\mc A_m=0$ or a kinetic relation
$\dot r_m=\mc K_m(\mc A_m,\text{state})$.  Thus, for kinetically limited
mechanisms such as slow fluid--solid reactions, \eqref{eq:el_conversion_affinity}
supplies the driving force rather than an algebraic equilibrium equation.  For an
instantaneous phase transformation, $\dot r_m$ may be a
measure in time.  In all cases, the phase momentum equations retain the inertial
production term and the $\nabla\mu_\xi^\alpha$ force generated by adding or
removing mass in a phase moving with velocity $\mbf v_\xi$.  For a single-component solid,
$\eta_{\xi_s}^\alpha=1$.

\subsection{Instantaneous phase-transformation jumps}
\label{sec:instantaneous_c_jump}

rate for the corresponding one-to-one stoichiometric mechanism.  If mechanism
$m$ moves component $\alpha$ from phase $\xi$ to phase $\zeta$ at $t=t_*$, we may
write
\begin{equation}
  \dot r_m = Q_m\,\delta(t-t_*),
  \label{eq:q_jump_decomp}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \dot c_\kappa^\beta
  = \nu_{\kappa m}^\beta Q_m\,\delta(t-t_*)
\end{equation}

through $t_*$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \left[\!\left[\phi_\kappa\bar\rho_\kappa\eta_\kappa^\beta\right]\!\right]_{t_*}

  \label{eq:phase_mass_jump_q}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mobile-phase momentum equations}
\label{sec:mobile_phase_momentum}

For the aqueous phase, the capillary contribution gives the intermediate form
\begin{align}
  \rho_a\mbf a_a
  &= \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma'_a + \rho_a\mbf g + \mbf b_a
  + \phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\lambda
  -\sum_\alpha\nabla_{\mbf x}\tau_a^\alpha
  -\phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a
  + \sum_\alpha \dot c_a^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_a^\alpha-\mbf{v}_a\right).
  \label{eq:el_mom_a_start}
\end{align}
The oleic and gas equations have the same form with $a$ replaced by $o$ or $g$.
The important point is that the interfacial variation contributes gradient terms
$\phi_\xi\nabla\gamma_\xi$, not merely algebraic multiples of
$\nabla\phi_\xi$.  These terms must be retained until the volume-fraction pressure relations are substituted.  After substituting the volume-fraction
Euler--Lagrange equations and using $\sum_\alpha\tau_\xi^\alpha=\phi_\xi\bar p_\xi$,
the multiplier and capillary-potential gradients combine with the pressure
potential gradients to give the standard phase pressure-gradient form
\begin{align}
  \rho_a\mbf{a}_a
  &= \rho_a\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_a
  - \phi_a\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_a
  + \sum_\alpha \dot c_a^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_a^\alpha-\mbf{v}_a\right),
  \label{eq:el_mom_a_simplified} \\
  \rho_o\mbf{a}_o
  &= \rho_o\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_o
  - \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_o
  + \sum_\alpha \dot c_o^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_o^\alpha-\mbf{v}_o\right),
  \label{eq:el_mom_o_simplified} \\
  \rho_g\mbf{a}_g
  &= \rho_g\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_g
  - \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_g
  + \sum_\alpha \dot c_g^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_g^\alpha-\mbf{v}_g\right).
  \label{eq:el_mom_g_simplified}
\end{align}

\subsection{Multicomponent solid skeleton and recovery of mixture rules}
\label{sec:multicomponent_solid_mixture_rules}

The present notation allows the solid skeleton to be treated as a single solid
phase, denoted here by $s$, while still resolving the mineral or solid components
through the mass fractions $\eta_s^\alpha$.  This is useful when reactions
convert one solid mineral into another but the products remain part of the same
load-bearing skeleton and share the same macroscopic deformation map
$\mbf x_s=\bs\chi_s(\mbf X_s,t)$.  In that case the solid free energy can be
chosen so that the component composition only enters through composition-weighted
moduli.  This recovers the mixture rules used in the earlier reacting-mixture
formulation, but avoids introducing a separate mechanical phase for each solid
mineral.

For the present fully compressible solid model we still need the distension
kinematics before it is meaningful to define an intrinsic stress.  The solid
phase has a macroscopic skeleton deformation $\mbf F_s$, but the stress carried
by a solid component is conjugate to the true, or undistended, deformation.  We
therefore introduce a solid distension tensor $\mbf A_s$ and write
\begin{equation}
  \mbf F_s=\mbf A_s\bar{\mbf F}_s,
  \qquad
  J_s=a_s\bar J_s,
  \qquad
  a_s=\det\mbf A_s,
  \qquad
  \bar J_s=\det\bar{\mbf F}_s .
  \label{eq:solid_distension_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Here $\bar{\mbf F}_s$ is the true deformation seen by the load-bearing solid
material, while $\mbf A_s$ accounts for the change in the relation between the
solid material volume and the mixture volume.  In the isotropic specialization
one may set $\mbf A_s=a_s^{1/3}\mbf I$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mbf F}_s=a_s^{-1/3}\mbf F_s .
  \label{eq:isotropic_distension_fbar}
\end{equation}
This is not the introduction of a separate distension energy.  It is the
kinematic device needed to separate deformation of the solid material from
changes in pore-space allocation, packing, and reaction-induced filling.  The
evolution of $a_s$ follows from the same mass-balance argument used by
Drumheller and in the earlier reacting-mixture formulation.  Summing the
component material balances over solid components gives the phase mass balance
\begin{equation}
  J_s\phi_s\bar\rho_s
  =\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}+c_s,
  \qquad
  c_s=\sum_\alpha c_s^\alpha .
  \label{eq:solid_material_mass_distension}
\end{equation}
Using $J_s=a_s\bar J_s$ and imposing true local conservation of mass for the
solid material,
\begin{equation}
  \bar J_s\bar\rho_s=\bar\rho_{s0},
  \label{eq:solid_true_mass_conservation}
\end{equation}
transforms \eqref{eq:solid_material_mass_distension} into
\begin{equation}
  a_s\phi_s\bar\rho_{s0}
  =\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}+c_s .
  \label{eq:solid_distension_mass_relation}
\end{equation}
Thus the solid volume fraction is tied directly to the motion only when there is
no mass exchange.  Taking the material time derivative of $J_s=a_s\bar J_s$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{D_s a_s/Dt}{a_s}
  =\frac{D_s J_s/Dt}{J_s}-\frac{D_s\bar J_s/Dt}{\bar J_s} .
\end{equation}
Since $D_sJ_s/Dt=J_s\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s$ and
\eqref{eq:solid_true_mass_conservation} implies
$(D_s\bar J_s/Dt)/\bar J_s=-(D_s\bar\rho_s/Dt)/\bar\rho_s$, the distension
evolution law is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{a_s}\frac{D_s a_s}{Dt}
  =\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s
  +\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s}\frac{D_s\bar\rho_s}{Dt} .
  \label{eq:solid_distension_evolution}
\end{equation}
For incompressible solid constituents, $D_s\bar\rho_s/Dt=0$, and therefore
$D_s a_s/Dt=a_s\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s$.  If additionally $a_s=J_s$ in the
reference state, then $a_s=J_s$ for all time; for compressible multicomponent
solids, however, the intrinsic-density term makes $a_s$ evolve independently of
$J_s$.

For non-isothermal problems we follow the thermal split used in finite-strain
thermo-hydro-mechanics, but apply it to the true deformation rather than to the
total skeleton deformation.  Thus
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mbf F}_s=\bar{\mbf F}_s^M\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta,
  \qquad
  \bar{\mbf F}_s^M=\bar{\mbf F}_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1},
  \label{eq:true_thermal_split}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta$ maps to the thermal stress-free true-solid
configuration and $\bar{\mbf F}_s^M$ is the mechanical part that enters the
intrinsic stresses.  For isotropic thermal expansion,
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta
  =\exp\left[\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\alpha_{\rm sk}(\vartheta,\eta_s^\beta)\,d\vartheta\right]\mbf I,
  \qquad
  \bar J_s^\theta
  =\exp\left[3\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\alpha_{\rm sk}(\vartheta,\eta_s^\beta)\,d\vartheta\right].
  \label{eq:thermal_expansion_split}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha_{\rm sk}$ is a skeleton thermal expansion coefficient that may depend
on the solid composition.  This is the single-skeleton analogue of assigning
different thermal expansion behavior to different minerals: the composition
updates $\alpha_{\rm sk}$, while all solid components remain attached to the same
true deformation field.

This thermal split does not break the energy-based structure.  It is a
constitutive reparametrization of the free energy: instead of depending on
$\bar{\mbf F}_s$ directly, the elastic part of the free energy depends on
$\bar{\mbf F}_s^M=\bar{\mbf F}_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1}$ and on $\theta$.  The
phase label remains part of the component Helmholtz energy: component $\alpha$ in
the solid phase need not have the same free energy as component $\alpha$ in a
liquid or gas phase.  The multicomponent solid free energy is written as
\begin{equation}
  \psi_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta)
  =\sum_\alpha \eta_s^\alpha\,
    \psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta),
  \label{eq:solid_multicomponent_free_energy}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_s^\beta$ denotes the full list of solid component mass fractions.
Temperature dependence of the free energy supplies entropy and heat capacity in
the usual way, while the dependence of $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta$ on $\theta$ and
$\eta_s^\beta$ encodes stress-free thermal expansion.  For example, water in a
hydrate or ice-like solid would be represented by $\psi_s^{\mathrm{H_2O}}$,
whereas liquid water would have a different function $\psi_\ell^{\mathrm{H_2O}}$.
Multiplying by the phase mass per mixture volume gives
\begin{equation}
  \phi_s\bar\rho_s\psi_s
  =\sum_\alpha \rho_s^\alpha
    \psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta),
  \qquad
  \rho_s^\alpha=\phi_s\bar\rho_s\eta_s^\alpha .
  \label{eq:solid_volume_energy_sum}
\end{equation}
If component volume fractions are used instead, then
\begin{equation}
  \rho_s^\alpha=\phi_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \phi_s\bar\rho_s=\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \eta_s^\alpha
  =\frac{\phi_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s^\alpha}{\phi_s\bar\rho_s} .
  \label{eq:solid_mass_volume_partitions}
\end{equation}
Reactions update $\eta_s^\alpha$, and therefore $\phi_s^\alpha$, through
\eqref{eq:solid_mass_volume_partitions}; the distension evolution
\eqref{eq:solid_distension_evolution} determines how much of that conversion
changes the true solid deformation.

The intrinsic stresses should not be postulated directly from the free energy in
this section, because $\bar\rho_s^\alpha$, $a_s$, $\theta$, and the composition may all
evolve.  Instead, define the component current Helmholtz energy density
\begin{equation}
  \bar w_s^\alpha
  =\bar\rho_s^\alpha\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta),
  \label{eq:intrinsic_current_energy_density}
\end{equation}
and let the Coleman--Noll procedure determine the admissible stress response.
Appendix~\ref{sec:coleman_noll_compositional} shows that the intrinsic Cauchy
stress is the coefficient of the mechanical true deformation rate and that the
phase stress is obtained only after projection through the distension kinematics.
At fixed $a_s$, the isotropic specialization takes the spatial mixture form
\begin{equation}
  \bs\sigma_s^{E}
  =\sum_\alpha \phi_s^\alpha\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \mbf P_s^{E}=J_s\bs\sigma_s^{E}\mbf F_s^{-T},
  \label{eq:elastic_phase_stress_from_intrinsic}
\end{equation}
while a general distension evolution adds the conjugate distension force coming
from variations of $a_s$ or $\mbf A_s$.  Thus
\begin{equation}
  \mbf P_s'
  =\mbf P_s^{E}+\mbf P_s^{a},
  \label{eq:phase_stress_intrinsic_plus_distension}
\end{equation}
where $\mbf P_s'$ is the effective phase stress in the Euler--Lagrange equations
and $\mbf P_s^{a}$ denotes the part generated by the distension closure.  This is
the precise sense in which the volume-fraction sum of intrinsic component
stresses is related to the phase stress; it is not an independent postulate.
Linearizing \eqref{eq:elastic_phase_stress_from_intrinsic} about a common true
strain gives the corresponding mixture rules for the true solid moduli,
\begin{equation}
  \bar G_{\rm eff}=\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha \bar G_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \bar K_{\rm eff}=\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha \bar K_s^\alpha .
  \label{eq:solid_modulus_mixture_rule_from_stress}
\end{equation}

The Biot coupling should be built from these true variables.  Introduce the
component intrinsic specific volume $\bar v_s^\alpha=1/\bar\rho_s^\alpha$ and
write $\psi_s^\alpha=\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s,\bar v_s^\alpha,a_s)$.  The
pressure conjugate to $\bar v_s^\alpha$ is
\begin{equation}
  \bar p_s^\alpha
  =-\left.\frac{\partial \psi_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}\right\vert_{\bar{\mbf F}_s,a_s}.
  \label{eq:component_intrinsic_pressure}
\end{equation}
The component Legendre transform is
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s,\bar p_s^\alpha,a_s)
  =\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s,\bar v_s^\alpha,a_s)
  +\bar p_s^\alpha\bar v_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \left.\frac{\partial\tilde\psi_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar p_s^\alpha}\right\vert_{\bar{\mbf F}_s,a_s}
  =\bar v_s^\alpha .
  \label{eq:component_legendre_transform}
\end{equation}
The fixed-pressure intrinsic stress is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mbf P}_s^{\alpha\prime\prime}
  =\bar{\mbf P}_s^\alpha
  -\bar p_s^\alpha\frac{1}{\bar v_{s0}^\alpha}
    \left.\frac{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar{\mbf F}_s}\right\vert_{\bar p_s^\alpha,a_s}.
  \label{eq:component_biot_stress_transform}
\end{equation}
If $\bar v_s^\alpha$ depends on the true volumetric deformation through
$\bar J_s$, then
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\mbf P}_s^\alpha
  =\bar{\mbf P}_s^{\alpha\prime\prime}
  +\left(1-B_s^\alpha\right)\bar p_s^\alpha \bar J_s\bar{\mbf F}_s^{-T},
  \qquad
  B_s^\alpha
  =1-\frac{1}{\bar v_{s0}^\alpha}
    \left.\frac{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar J_s}\right\vert_{\bar p_s^\alpha,a_s} .
  \label{eq:component_generalized_biot}
\end{equation}
Projecting this relation through \eqref{eq:solid_distension_decomposition} gives
the Biot term in the phase stress.  In the present variational formulation the
scalar multiplying the projected $J_s\mbf F_s^{-T}$ term is the saturation
multiplier $\lambda$, with the conventional compressive pressure given by
\begin{equation}
  p=-\lambda .
  \label{eq:lambda_pressure_identification}
\end{equation}
Consequently a single connected pore pressure gives, in this notation,
\begin{equation}
  \mbf P_s' + \lambda J_s\mbf F_s^{-T}
  =\mbf P_s^{\prime\prime}+B_s^{\rm eff}\lambda J_s\mbf F_s^{-T},
  \qquad
  B_s^{\rm eff}=\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha B_s^\alpha,
  \label{eq:effective_biot_mixture_rule}
\end{equation}
up to the same distension-projection terms collected in $\mbf P_s^a$.  Replacing
$\lambda$ by $-p$ recovers the conventional Biot notation.  In the one-component
solid/one-fluid limit with trivial distension projection, this reduces to
\begin{equation}
  B=1-\frac{1}{v_{s0}}\left.\frac{\partial \bar v_s}{\partial \bar J_s}\right\vert_{-\lambda},
\end{equation}
which gives $B=1-K/K_s$ in the small-strain limit.

\subsection{Constitutive specialization: crystallization-driven distension}
\label{sec:constitutive_crystallization_distension}

The preceding development leaves the distension response abstract through the
closure \eqref{eq:solid_distension_evolution}.  We now specialize that closure to
a precipitating solid component by separating three ingredients: the mechanical
penalty for changing the true solid volume, the chemical driving force for
crystallization, and the resulting load on the distension coordinate.

The volumetric decomposition is
\begin{equation}
  J_s=a_s\bar J_s,
  \qquad
  \bar J_s=\frac{J_s}{a_s},
  \label{eq:crystallization_volumetric_split}
\end{equation}
where $a_s=\det\mbf A_s$ is the distension volume ratio and $\bar J_s$ is the
true solid volume ratio.  A simple elastic penalty for changing the true solid
volume of component $\alpha$ is
\begin{equation}
  W_{\rm mech}^\alpha(J_s,a_s)
  =\frac{K_s^\alpha}{2}
  \left[\ln\left(\frac{J_s}{a_s}\right)\right]^2,
  \label{eq:crystallization_mechanical_energy}
\end{equation}
with intrinsic bulk modulus $K_s^\alpha$.  This term vanishes when
$\bar J_s=1$: distension itself is not penalized unless, at the prescribed
skeleton deformation $J_s$, it changes the true solid volume.

Crystallization enters as a chemical loading on $a_s$.  The variational theory
has already identified the generalized force $\mc A_m$ conjugate to the mechanism
progress rate $\dot r_m$ through
\begin{equation}
  \delta W_{\rm conv}
  =-\sum_m\int_{\mc B}\mc A_m\,\delta\dot r_m\,\dv,
  \qquad
  \mc A_m=-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha .
  \label{eq:crystallization_affinity_projection_recall}
\end{equation}
Because the source law is mass-stoichiometric,
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m$, the affinity $\mc A_m$ is
mass specific.  For a mechanism $m$ precipitating solid component $\alpha_m$,
the corresponding crystallization pressure is therefore
\begin{equation}
  p_c^{(m)}
  =\frac{\mc A_m}{\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}}
  =\bar\rho_s^{\alpha_m}\mc A_m,
  \qquad
  \bar v_s^{\alpha_m}=\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s^{\alpha_m}} .
  \label{eq:crystallization_pressure_affinity_mass_specific}
\end{equation}
If one instead uses a molar affinity
$\mc A_m^{\rm mol}=M_s^{\alpha_m}\mc A_m$, then
\begin{equation}
  p_c^{(m)}
  =\frac{\mc A_m^{\rm mol}}{V_s^{\alpha_m}}
  =\frac{R\theta}{V_s^{\alpha_m}}\ln\Omega_m,
  \label{eq:classical_crystallization_pressure}
\end{equation}
which recovers the classical supersaturation formula.  Thus $\Omega_m$ is a
possible chemical closure for the affinity; it is not an additional variational
coordinate.

The crystallization-loaded distension energy is then
\begin{equation}
  W_a^\alpha(J_s,a_s,\mc A_m)
  =W_{\rm mech}^\alpha(J_s,a_s)-p_c^{(m)}\ln a_s
  =\frac{K_s^\alpha}{2}
  \left[\ln\left(\frac{J_s}{a_s}\right)\right]^2
  -p_c^{(m)}\ln a_s .
  \label{eq:crystallization_distension_energy}
\end{equation}
The logarithm is natural for the positive multiplicative variable $a_s$ and is
normalized so that the chemical work vanishes at $a_s=1$.

The scalar distension pressure generated by
\eqref{eq:crystallization_distension_energy} is the force conjugate to $a_s$ at
fixed skeleton deformation and chemical driving force:
\begin{equation}
  p_a^\alpha
  =-\left.\frac{\partial W_a^\alpha}{\partial a_s}\right\vert_{J_s,\mc A_m}
  =\frac{1}{a_s}\left[
  K_s^\alpha\ln\left(\frac{J_s}{a_s}\right)+p_c^{(m)}\right].
  \label{eq:distension_pressure_gradient}
\end{equation}
Equilibrium with respect to distension therefore requires
\begin{equation}
  K_s^\alpha\ln\bar J_s+p_c^{(m)}=0 .
  \label{eq:distension_equilibrium_crystallization}
\end{equation}
In this form the interpretation is direct: supersaturation, through the affinity
$\mc A_m$, supplies a pressure $p_c^{(m)}$ that biases the otherwise elastic
relaxation of the true solid volume.

\paragraph{Crystallization Biot coefficient.}
Because $p_c^{(m)}=\bar\rho_s^{\alpha_m}\mc A_m$ depends on the intrinsic density
of the precipitating solid component, the crystallization loading also enters
the component intrinsic-pressure response.  A Biot-type coefficient associated
with this pressure should therefore be defined after a Legendre transformation
with respect to the component specific volume.  The variables held fixed are
important: holding $a_s$ fixed would suppress the distension mechanism.  For
crystallization-driven distension the relevant derivative holds the total
skeleton deformation fixed, equivalently $J_s$ in the volumetric specialization:
\begin{equation}
  B_{c,s}^{\alpha}
  =1-\frac{1}{\bar v_{s0}^\alpha}
  \left.
  \frac{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar J_s}
  \right|_{\bar p_s^\alpha,\mbf F_s,\mc A_m},
  \label{eq:crystallization_biot_coefficient}
\end{equation}
or, for purely volumetric variations,
\begin{equation}
  \left.
  \frac{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar J_s}
  \right|_{\bar p_s^\alpha,\mbf F_s,\mc A_m}
  \equiv
  \left.
  \frac{\partial \bar v_s^\alpha}{\partial \bar J_s}
  \right|_{\bar p_s^\alpha,J_s,\mc A_m} .
  \label{eq:crystallization_biot_fixed_F_equiv}
\end{equation}
At fixed $\mbf F_s$, increasing $\bar J_s$ requires the compensating change
$a_s=J_s/\bar J_s$; this is precisely the crystallization--distension coupling
measured by $B_{c,s}^\alpha$.
\subsection{Classical compositional-flow limit and interpretation of the multipliers}
\label{sec:classical_compositional_limit}

The preceding equations contain the usual compositional-reservoir structure as a
special limit.  The phase/component source is written as
\begin{equation}
  \dot c_\xi^\alpha=\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m,
  \label{eq:classical_source_form}
\end{equation}
which is the phase-resolved analogue of the standard reactive-transport source
vector $\mbf R=\bs\nu\mbf q$.  Summing \eqref{eq:spatial_mass_2} over phases gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\right)
  +\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\mbf v_\xi\right)
  =\sum_\xi\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m .
  \label{eq:classical_component_balance}
\end{equation}
This is the conservative component balance used in compositional reservoir
simulation, except that the stoichiometric coefficients retain the phase label.
That phase label permits a single mechanism to encode both chemical conversion
and the phase in which the product appears.  A pure phase transformation is the
special case in which the component label is unchanged and the two nonzero
stoichiometric entries are equal and opposite.

The multiplier $\mu_\xi^\alpha$ in \eqref{eq:C4_def} is the chemical-potential-like
variable conjugate to phase/component conversion.  Its evolution equation is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
  =\frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
  -\psi_\xi
  -\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha} .
  \label{eq:mu_interpretation}
\end{equation}
The first term is the kinetic contribution associated with adding mass to a
phase moving with velocity $\mbf v_\xi$.  The second is the Helmholtz free energy
carried by the phase.  The last term is the contribution of the component mass
constraint; $\tau_\xi^\alpha$ is the multiplier enforcing the storage relation
between the reference amount of component $\alpha$ and the current partial
mass density $\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha$.  Thus
$D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha/Dt$ is a generalized chemical-mechanical potential.  In a
quasi-static isothermal limit with negligible kinetic terms and a conventional
choice of thermodynamic potential, this quantity reduces to the chemical
potential (or, equivalently in reservoir practice, to a fugacity/equilibrium
potential) that appears in phase and reaction equilibrium conditions.

The affinity is the stoichiometric projection of these phase/component
potentials:
\begin{equation}
  \mc A_m +\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=0 .
  \label{eq:classical_affinity_projection}
\end{equation}
For an equilibrated mechanism, imposing $\mc A_m=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=0,
  \label{eq:classical_equilibrium_affinity}
\end{equation}
which is the classical statement that the stoichiometric sum of chemical
potentials vanishes.  For a kinetically limited mechanism, such as a slow
fluid--solid reaction, \eqref{eq:classical_affinity_projection} instead supplies
the driving force in a closure
\begin{equation}
  \dot r_m=\mc K_m(\mc A_m,\text{state}),
  \qquad \mc A_m\dot r_m\ge 0
\end{equation}
with the sign convention used here.  Thus equilibrium chemistry, kinetic
reactive transport, and phase transformation all use the same stoichiometric
projection; only the closure for $\dot r_m$ changes.

The momentum equations contain an additional term absent from purely kinematic
Darcy-scale compositional balances,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_\alpha \dot c_\xi^\alpha\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
  \label{eq:conversion_momentum_interpretation}
\end{equation}
The factor $-\dot c_\xi^\alpha\mbf v_\xi$ is the inertial bookkeeping required
when mass is removed from or added to a phase moving with velocity $\mbf v_\xi$.
The gradient term comes from the variation of the current-rate constraint
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$ and is therefore the mechanical force
associated with spatial changes in the conversion potential.  These terms vanish
or are hidden in classical reservoir models when phase momenta are not solved
explicitly and the phase velocities are supplied by Darcy laws.

Finally, the remaining multipliers have standard constraint interpretations:
$\lambda$ enforces saturation of the pore volume by the phases,
$\pi_\xi$ enforces $\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1$, and $\tau_\xi^\alpha$ enforces
the phase/component storage relation.  The novelty of the present formulation is
not the stoichiometric source form itself, but the fact that it is embedded in a
phase-attached variational mechanics in which the Jacobian $J_\xi$ is varied.
That is what exposes the conversion-induced momentum production terms and gives
a direct bridge between mixture mechanics and classical compositional reactive
transport.

\section{Coleman--Noll procedure for the compositional thermo-reactive mixture}
\label{sec:coleman_noll_compositional}

This appendix records the thermodynamic argument in a form that mirrors the
kinematic split used in the body of the paper.  The point is not to postulate the
solid mixture rules, but to show how they appear after the component Helmholtz
energies are differentiated subject to the density, composition, and distension
constraints.

For each phase/component pair let
\begin{equation}
  e_\xi^\alpha=\psi_\xi^\alpha+\theta s_\xi^\alpha
  \label{eq:CN_legendre}
\end{equation}
be the Legendre relation.  The primitive Helmholtz free energies are component
specific, but the volumetric state of a phase is the phase intrinsic density.  Thus
specific, but the volumetric state of a phase is the phase intrinsic density.  For
the solid, component additivity is retained while all components share the same
phase density and true deformation:
\begin{align}
  \psi_s^\alpha
  &=\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M(\theta, \eta^\beta_\xi), \mbf{A}_s,
  \bar\rho_s,\mc A_m),
  \qquad
  \bar{\mbf F}_s^M=\bar{\mbf F}_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1},
  \label{eq:CN_component_free_energy_arguments}
\end{align}
Here $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta=\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta(\theta,\eta_s^\beta)$ is a
prescribed stress-free thermal distortion, not an independent thermodynamic
state.  The dependent quantities
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\xi^\alpha=\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1,
  \qquad
  \bar J_s=\det\bar{\mbf F}_s=\frac{J_s}{a_s}
  \label{eq:CN_kinematic_dependencies}
\end{equation}
are substituted before the entropy inequality is reduced.  In the solid phase we
now regard the volume fraction as a function of the full distension tensor,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_s=\phi_s(\mbf A_s),
  \qquad
  \dot\phi_s=\frac{\partial\phi_s}{\partial\mbf A_s}:\dot{\mbf A}_s .
  \label{eq:CN_phi_A_definition}
\end{equation}  For the solid, component
additivity gives
\begin{equation}
  \Psi_s=\sum_\alpha\rho_s^\alpha\psi_s^\alpha
  =\rho_s\sum_\alpha\eta_s^\alpha\psi_s^\alpha,
  \qquad
  \psi_s=\sum_\alpha\eta_s^\alpha\psi_s^\alpha .
  \label{eq:CN_total_helmholtz_density}
\end{equation}
The mobile-phase Helmholtz energies are not assumed to be component-additive;
they are phase EOS potentials $\rho_\ell\psi_\ell(\bar\rho_\ell,\eta_\ell^\beta,\theta)$.
\begin{align}
  \sum_\alpha\left(\rho_\xi^\alpha\dot e_\xi^\alpha
  +\dot c_\xi^\alpha e_\xi^\alpha\right)
  =&\; \bs\sigma_\xi':\mbf L_\xi
  +\phi_\xi\bar p_\xi\frac{\dot{\bar\rho}_\xi}{\bar\rho_\xi}
  -\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf q_\xi
  +\rho_\xi r_\xi+\rho_\xi e_\xi^{\rm int}.
  \label{eq:CN_energy_balance}
\end{align}
For the solid phase, the mechanical power in \eqref{eq:CN_energy_balance} must
be decomposed with the same kinematic split used in the constitutive law.  In the
isotropic distension specialization,
\begin{equation}
  \mbf F_s=a_s^{1/3}\bar{\mbf F}_s^M\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta,
  \qquad
  \mbf L_s=\dot{\mbf F}_s\mbf F_s^{-1} .
  \label{eq:CN_F_split_for_power}
\end{equation}
For the reduced mechanical Coleman--Noll collection we hold the prescribed
stress-free distortion fixed, so that the independent solid kinematic rates are
$\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M$, $\dot a_s$, and $\dot{\bar\rho}_s$.  Taking the
material time derivative and multiplying by $\mbf F_s^{-1}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \mbf L_s
  =\frac{1}{3}\frac{\dot a_s}{a_s}\mbf I
  +\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^{-1} .
  \label{eq:CN_L_split_for_power}
\end{equation}
Therefore the solid stress power contains only the reduced rates
\begin{equation}
  \bs\sigma_s':\mbf L_s
  =\left[\bs\sigma_s'(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^{-T}\right]
  :\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M
  +\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')\frac{\dot a_s}{a_s} .
  \label{eq:CN_stress_power_split}
\end{equation}
The trace of \eqref{eq:CN_L_split_for_power} and the distension evolution law
then imply
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{tr}\mbf L_s
  &=\frac{\dot a_s}{a_s}
  +\operatorname{tr}\left[\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M
  (\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^{-1}\right],
  \label{eq:CN_trace_L_in_terms_of_FM}\\
  \frac{\dot a_s}{a_s}
  &=\operatorname{tr}\mbf L_s+\frac{\dot{\bar\rho}_s}{\bar\rho_s},
  \label{eq:CN_distension_rate_substitution}
\end{align}
so the reduced rates satisfy the intrinsic-density constraint
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}\left[\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M
  (\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^{-1}\right]
  =-\frac{\dot{\bar\rho}_s}{\bar\rho_s} .
  \label{eq:CN_density_constraint_from_trace}
\end{equation}
Hence no occurrence of $\mbf L_s$ is needed in the constitutive stress power:
\eqref{eq:CN_stress_power_split}, together with the constraint
\eqref{eq:CN_density_constraint_from_trace}, is a power written entirely in
terms of $\bar{\mbf F}_s^M$, $a_s$, $\bar\rho_s$ and their rates.  Consequently
$\bs\sigma_s'$ may be specified as a constitutive function of only these reduced
state variables, rather than of the macroscopic deformation gradient or velocity
gradient.
\begin{equation}
  0\le
  \sum_\xi\left[\sum_\alpha\left(
  \rho_\xi^\alpha\dot s_\xi^\alpha
  +\dot c_\xi^\alpha s_\xi^\alpha\right)
  +\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\frac{\mbf q_\xi}{\theta}\right)
  -\frac{\rho_\xi r_\xi}{\theta}\right].
  \label{eq:CN_entropy_inequality}
\end{equation}
The conversion virtual power is the one used in the variational derivation:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_m\mc A_m\dot r_m
  +\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\dot c_\xi^\alpha
  =\sum_m\left(\mc A_m+
  \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)
  \dot r_m .
  \label{eq:CN_composition_affinity_projection}
\end{equation}
After substituting \eqref{eq:CN_legendre} and
\eqref{eq:CN_energy_balance} into \eqref{eq:CN_entropy_inequality}, the
reversible part of the reduced inequality contains the mechanical and pressure
powers minus $\dot\Psi$, plus the conversion term
\eqref{eq:CN_composition_affinity_projection}.

The trace of \eqref{eq:CN_L_split_for_power}, together with true solid mass
conservation, gives
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}\left[\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^{-1}\right]
  +\operatorname{tr}\left[\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^\theta
  (\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1}\right]
  =-\frac{\dot{\bar\rho}_s}{\bar\rho_s} .
  \label{eq:CN_true_density_rate_constraint}
\end{equation}
Thus the reversible solid rates may be collected in terms of
$\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M$, $\dot{\bar\rho}_s$, and $\dot\eta_s^\alpha$, with
$\dot a_s/a_s$ eliminated by \eqref{eq:CN_distension_rate_substitution} when the
macroscopic volume rate is used.  The component free-energy rate is
\begin{equation}
  \dot\psi_s^\alpha
  =
  \frac{\partial\psi_s^\alpha}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}_s^M}
  :
  \dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M
  +
  \frac{\partial\psi_s^\alpha}{\partial\bar\rho_s}\dot{\bar\rho}_s
  +
  \frac{\partial\psi_s^\alpha}{\partial a_s}\dot a_s
  +
  \frac{\partial\psi_s^\alpha}{\partial\mc A_m}\dot{\mc A}_m .
  \label{eq:CN_solid_rate_expanded}
\end{equation}
Here $\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}_s^M$ includes the induced rates of the prescribed
thermal distortion $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta(\theta,\eta_s^\beta)$.  This is where
the split appears: the free energy is differentiated with respect to the true
mechanical deformation and the distension variable separately, while the
kinematic relation above determines how their conjugate forces combine in the
macroscopic solid power.

Collecting the coefficients of the independent reversible rates gives the solid
mixture rules.  In the isotropic distension specialization, the power conjugate
to $\dot a_s$ contributes a spherical part to the macroscopic stress because
$\partial\mbf F_s/\partial a_s=(3a_s)^{-1}\mbf F_s$ at fixed
$\bar{\mbf F}_s$.  Up to capillary terms,
\begin{align}
  \mbf P_s^E
  =&\;J_s a_s^{-1/3}
  \frac{\partial\Psi_s}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}_s^M}:
  \frac{\partial\bar{\mbf F}_s^M}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}_s}
  +\frac{J_s}{3a_s}\frac{\partial\Psi_s}{\partial a_s}\mbf F_s^{-T}
  +\mbf P_{\gamma s} .
  \label{eq:CN_stress_definition}
\end{align}
The phase intrinsic pressure conjugate to $\dot{\bar\rho}_s/\bar\rho_s$ is
\begin{equation}
  \phi_s\bar p_s
  =\bar\rho_s\frac{\partial\Psi_s}{\partial\bar\rho_s}
  +a_s\frac{\partial\Psi_s}{\partial a_s}
  +\Pi_{\gamma\rho}^s .
  \label{eq:CN_phase_pressure_definition}
\end{equation}
\eqref{eq:CN_phase_pressure_definition} are the Coleman--Noll version of the
solid split derived earlier: the true deformation, distension, and intrinsic
density are thermodynamically conjugate to different parts of the reversible
power, but the kinematic constraint couples their apparent contributions.

The component mixture rules come from the coefficients of
$\dot\eta_\xi^\alpha$ subject to $\sum_\alpha\dot\eta_\xi^\alpha=0$.  Since
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\Psi_\xi}{\partial\eta_\xi^\alpha}
  =\rho_\xi\left(
  \psi_\xi^\alpha+
  \sum_\beta\eta_\xi^\beta
  \left.\frac{\partial\psi_\xi^\beta}{\partial\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right|_{\mc Z_\eta}
  \right),
  \label{eq:CN_composition_derivative}
\end{equation}
only differences of these coefficients are independent.  The arbitrary common
part is the Coleman--Noll counterpart of the phase multiplier $\pi_\xi$ in the
variational calculation.  Thus the phase free energy
$\psi_\xi=\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha\psi_\xi^\alpha$, the composition forces, and
the corresponding component mixture rules are not separate assumptions; they are
produced by differentiating the component-additive storage law under the mass
fraction constraint.

The entropy is identified from the coefficient of $\dot\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  s=-\left.\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta}\right|_{\mc Z} .
  \label{eq:CN_entropy_definitions}
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $\dot{\mc A}_m$ records reversible storage associated with
crystallization/distension.  For the specialization
$W_a^\alpha=W_{\rm mech}^\alpha-p_c^{(m)}\ln a_s$, with
$p_c^{(m)}=\bar\rho_s^{\alpha_m}\mc A_m$, the derivative
$\partial\Psi_s/\partial a_s$ contains the crystallization pressure contribution.
The equilibrium condition obtained by setting the distension force to zero is
therefore the same condition derived in the body of the paper, while the Biot
coefficient introduced after the Legendre transform converts this local
conjugate force into an effective poromechanical property.

The remaining, nonnegative part of the reduced inequality is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal D
  =\sum_m\left(
  \mc A_m+
  \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)\dot r_m
  +\sum_\xi\rho_\xi e_\xi^{\rm int}
  -\sum_\xi\frac{\mbf q_\xi\cdot\nabla_{\mbf x}\theta}{\theta}
  +\mathcal D_{\rm inelastic}
  \ge0 .
  \label{eq:CN_residual_dissipation}
\end{equation}
In the reversible equilibrated limit the affinity projection removes the first
term.  Kinetic crystallization, heat conduction, interphase exchange, plasticity,
damage, and other rate-dependent effects must be specified so that the remaining
terms are nonnegative.
