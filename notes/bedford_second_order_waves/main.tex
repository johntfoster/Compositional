\begin{abstract}
Second-order wave equations for saturated porous media generally form a
coupled system rather than one scalar nonlinear-acoustic equation.  Even the
linear isotropic problem has fast and slow compressional modes and a shear
mode.  At second order, finite-deformation kinematics, the third derivatives of
the stored energy, the state dependence of the Biot coefficient, fluid storage,
inertia or tortuosity, and drag all generate quadratic forcing and mode
conversion.  The analysis first states the coupled second-order system and then
specializes it to one-dimensional compressional motion.  The specialization
shows explicitly how a finite-deformation Biot coefficient \(B(J)\) enters both
the linear wave speeds through \(B_0=B(1)\) and the quadratic wave coefficient
through \(B_1=(\partial B/\partial\ln J)_0\).  Projection onto one isolated
mode gives a Burgers-type amplitude equation and an explicit initial
second-harmonic prediction.  The calculation is a meaningful bridge to the
Hamilton--Blackstock program, while also showing why a porous medium generally
has a family of modal nonlinear coefficients rather than one universal
\(B/A\).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Second-order nonlinear wave equations and second-harmonic solutions have been
developed for several special forms of Biot theory.  Donskoy, Khashanah, and
McKee related an effective nonlinear parameter to porosity-dependent
structural properties
\cite{donskoy1997}.  Dazel and Tournat used successive approximations to derive
second-harmonic Biot waves when the principal quadratic nonlinearity is in the
solid frame \cite{dazel2010}.  Tong and coauthors retained coupled solid--fluid
nonlinearities in a Lagrangian formulation and obtained double-frequency mode
coupling \cite{tong2017}.  Berjamin studied fully nonlinear plane waves for a
more restricted mixture with incompressible constituents, a nonlinear elastic
skeleton, and tortuosity \cite{berjamin2021}.  Experiments on an air-saturated
granular slab have measured both self-action and second-harmonic generation and
found agreement with a nonlinear Biot model \cite{legland2012}.

What is not canonical is a single porous counterpart of the fluid
Westervelt or Burgers equation.  A saturated porous medium has at least two
compressional polarizations, and their relative solid--fluid motion depends on
frequency and drag.  Quadratic forcing can therefore steepen one mode, generate
its second harmonic, or transfer energy between fast and slow modes.  The most
natural nonlinear parameters are consequently modal projection coefficients.
They become material numbers only after the equilibrium state, constitutive
functions, frequency regime, mode normalization, and amplitude variable have
all been specified.

The following calculation gives a self-contained route from
finite-deformation poromechanics to those coefficients.  It also carries a
state-dependent, finite-deformation Biot coefficient into the weakly nonlinear
wave reduction.

\section{Finite-deformation parent quantities}

Consider one solid skeleton and one saturating barotropic fluid.  Reactions,
phase transformations, temperature changes, gravity, capillarity, and molecular
diffusion are omitted.  The reference state is homogeneous and in mechanical
equilibrium; it may be prestressed, but all coefficients below are evaluated at
that same state.

Let \(\mbf X\) label the solid reference configuration and write
\begin{equation}
    \mbf x=\mbf X+\mbf u(\mbf X,t),
    \qquad
    \mbf F=\mbf I+\nabla_{\mbf X}\mbf u,
    \qquad
    J=\det\mbf F,
    \qquad
    \mbf v_s=\dot{\mbf u}.
    \label{eq:solid_kinematics}
\end{equation}
The current and reference relative fluid mass fluxes are
\begin{equation}
    \mbf w_f
    =\phi_f\bar\rho_f\left(\mbf v_f-\mbf v_s\right),
    \qquad
    \mbf W_f=J\mbf F^{-1}\mbf w_f.
    \label{eq:relative_mass_fluxes}
\end{equation}
Here \(\phi_f\) is porosity, \(\bar\rho_f\) is intrinsic fluid density,
and \(\bar p_f\) is pore pressure.  The symbol \(\theta\) below denotes
temperature.  The exact Lagrangian fluid mass balance is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t}
    \left(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
    +\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f=0.
    \label{eq:exact_lagrangian_mass}
\end{equation}
Thus the storage variable is \(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\), not an independently
postulated small-strain fluid-content field.

For a single skeleton, the total stress has the fixed-pressure split
\begin{equation}
    \bs\sigma
    =\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}-B\bar p_f\mbf I.
    \label{eq:biot_stress_split}
\end{equation}
For the present single-skeleton specialization, the finite-deformation Biot
coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    B=1-\frac{1}{v_0}
    \left.
    \frac{\partial\bar v}{\partial J}
    \right|_{\bar p_f,\theta},
    \qquad
    v_0=\frac{1}{\rho_{s0}},
    \qquad
    \bar v=\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s}.
    \label{eq:nonlinear_biot_coefficient}
\end{equation}
The coefficient is a current-state tangent evaluated at fixed pore pressure
and temperature.  It reduces to the classical small-deformation Biot
coefficient but may vary with \(J\), pressure, composition, or internal state
\cite{fosterxu2025}.

For a barotropic fluid, a standard tangent form of
\eqref{eq:exact_lagrangian_mass} is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{B}{J}\dot J
    +\frac{1}{M}\dot{\bar p}_f
    +\frac{1}{\bar\rho_f}
    \nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f=0,
    \label{eq:finite_storage}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) is the storage modulus.  Equation
\eqref{eq:finite_storage} is exact only to the order represented by the
constitutive tangents \(B\) and \(M\); a second-order calculation must expand
those tangents and the density factor at the equilibrium state.  This point is
important because using a constant \(B\) in a nominally nonlinear wave model
would discard part of the quadratic solid--fluid coupling.

\section{Second-order structure in three dimensions}

Introduce a bookkeeping parameter \(\varepsilon\) proportional to wave
amplitude and write
\begin{align}
    \mbf u
    & =\varepsilon\mbf u^{(1)}
    +\varepsilon^2\mbf u^{(2)}+O\left(\varepsilon^3\right),
    \notag\\
    \bar p_f-\bar p_{f0}
    & =\varepsilon p^{(1)}
    +\varepsilon^2p^{(2)}+O\left(\varepsilon^3\right),
    \notag\\
    \mbf W_f
    & =\varepsilon\mbf W_f^{(1)}
    +\varepsilon^2\mbf W_f^{(2)}+O\left(\varepsilon^3\right).
    \label{eq:field_expansions}
\end{align}
The Jacobian has the expansion
\begin{align}
    J
    & =1+\varepsilon J^{(1)}+\varepsilon^2J^{(2)}
    +O\left(\varepsilon^3\right),
    \notag\\
    J^{(1)}
    & =\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf u^{(1)},
    \notag\\
    J^{(2)}
    & =\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf u^{(2)}
    +\frac{1}{2}
    \left[
    \left(\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf u^{(1)}\right)^2
    -\tr\left(\left(\nabla_{\mbf X}\mbf u^{(1)}\right)^2\right)
    \right].
    \label{eq:jacobian_expansion}
\end{align}
Expanding \(B\) about the equilibrium state gives, for example,
\begin{equation}
    B
    =B_0+\varepsilon
    \left(B_J\,J^{(1)}+B_p\,p^{(1)}\right)
    +O\left(\varepsilon^2\right),
    \label{eq:biot_expansion}
\end{equation}
where \(B_J=(\partial B/\partial J)_0\) and
\(B_p=(\partial B/\partial\bar p_f)_0\).  The definition
\eqref{eq:nonlinear_biot_coefficient} shows that \(B_J\) contains a second
derivative of the intrinsic solid specific volume:
\begin{equation}
    B_J
    =-\frac{1}{v_0}
    \left.
    \frac{\partial^2\bar v}{\partial J^2}
    \right|_0.
    \label{eq:biot_slope_specific_volume}
\end{equation}

Substitution of \eqref{eq:field_expansions} into the momentum balances,
\eqref{eq:exact_lagrangian_mass}, and the constitutive relations gives a
homogeneous linear system at order \(\varepsilon\).  At order
\(\varepsilon^2\), the unknown second-order solid motion, relative fluid
motion, and pressure occur through exactly the same linear combinations, while
quadratic products of the first-order solution appear as known forcing.  Those
quadratic terms fall into five physically distinct groups:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item finite-strain geometry, including \eqref{eq:jacobian_expansion} and
    stress transformations;
    \item third derivatives of the skeleton and fluid stored energies;
    \item state dependence of \(B\), \(M\), porosity, and intrinsic density;
    \item state dependence of inertia and tortuosity; and
    \item nonlinear or frequency-dependent drag.
\end{enumerate}
This repeated-linear-operator structure is the general answer at second order.
It is a coupled forced system, not yet a scalar Westervelt equation.  A scalar
evolution law follows only after a propagation direction, mode, frequency
regime, and one-way approximation are selected.

\section{Explicit one-dimensional compressional system}

The role of \(B(J)\) can be seen without carrying every three-dimensional
tensor.  Let \(X\) be the propagation coordinate, \(u(X,t)\) the longitudinal
solid displacement, and define a relative-fluid displacement \(z(X,t)\) by
\begin{equation}
    W_f=\bar\rho_{f0}\dot z.
    \label{eq:relative_displacement_definition}
\end{equation}
This \(z\) is a wave-reduction coordinate; it is not the relative mass flux
\(w_f\) in \eqref{eq:relative_mass_fluxes}.  Let
\(e=u_{,X}\), so \(J=1+e\).  A minimal dynamic Biot closure is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:oned_parent}
\begin{align}
    \rho_{11}u_{,tt}+\rho_{12}z_{,tt}
    & =\partial_X\left[P^{\prime\prime}(e)-B(J,\bar p_f)\bar p_f\right],
    \label{eq:oned_total_momentum}\\
    \rho_{12}u_{,tt}+\rho_{22}z_{,tt}+b_0z_{,t}
    & =-\partial_X\bar p_f,
    \label{eq:oned_relative_momentum}\\
    \frac{B(J,\bar p_f)}{J}J_{,t}
    +\frac{1}{M(J,\bar p_f)}\bar p_{f,t}
    +\frac{\bar\rho_{f0}}{\bar\rho_f(\bar p_f)}z_{,Xt}
    & =0.
    \label{eq:oned_storage}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The symmetric inertia coefficients must satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{11}>0,
    \qquad
    \rho_{22}>0,
    \qquad
    \rho_{11}\rho_{22}-\rho_{12}^2>0.
    \label{eq:inertia_positivity}
\end{equation}
They include the equilibrium phase masses and, when required by the frequency
range, added-mass or tortuosity effects.
The coefficient \(b_0\geq0\) is the linear drag.  Dynamic tortuosity and
permeability generally make both inertia and drag frequency dependent; constant
coefficients are used here only to expose the second-order constitutive terms.

To keep the displayed coefficients transparent, take
\(\bar p_{f0}=0\) for the explicit calculation and absorb the equilibrium
stress into the reference state.  A small-on-large calculation about
\(\bar p_{f0}\ne0\) has the same structure, but the linear stress tangent also
contains \(-\bar p_{f0}B_J\) and \(-\bar p_{f0}B_p\) contributions.  Define
\begin{equation}
    P^{\prime\prime}(e)
    =H e+\frac{1}{2}H_2e^2+O\left(e^3\right),
    \qquad
    B=B_0+B_J e+B_p p+O\left(\varepsilon^2\right).
    \label{eq:oned_constitutive_expansions}
\end{equation}
Here \(H\) is the tangent fixed-pressure longitudinal skeleton modulus and
\(H_2\) is its effective third-order modulus in the selected strain and stress
measures.  For clarity, first take \(M=M_0\), constant inertia, and constant
drag.  The first-order equations are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:first_order_system}
\begin{align}
    \rho_{11}u^{(1)}_{,tt}+\rho_{12}z^{(1)}_{,tt}
    -\partial_X\left(H e^{(1)}-B_0p^{(1)}\right)
    & =0,
    \label{eq:first_order_total_momentum}\\
    \rho_{12}u^{(1)}_{,tt}+\rho_{22}z^{(1)}_{,tt}
    +b_0z^{(1)}_{,t}+p^{(1)}_{,X}
    & =0,
    \label{eq:first_order_relative_momentum}\\
    B_0e^{(1)}_{,t}+\frac{1}{M_0}p^{(1)}_{,t}
    +z^{(1)}_{,Xt}
    & =0.
    \label{eq:first_order_storage}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
For zero initial perturbations, \eqref{eq:first_order_storage} gives
\begin{equation}
    p^{(1)}=-M_0\left(B_0u^{(1)}_{,X}+z^{(1)}_{,X}\right).
    \label{eq:first_order_pressure_elimination}
\end{equation}
In the lossless case, a plane wave with speed \(c\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \det\left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
        H+B_0^2M_0&B_0M_0\\
        B_0M_0&M_0
    \end{bmatrix}
    -c^2
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \rho_{11}&\rho_{12}\\
        \rho_{12}&\rho_{22}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right)=0.
    \label{eq:compressional_eigenproblem}
\end{equation}
The two roots are the fast and slow compressional speeds.  With drag, the
frequency-domain dispersion relation becomes
\begin{equation}
    \det\left[
    k^2
    \begin{bmatrix}
        H+B_0^2M_0&B_0M_0\\
        B_0M_0&M_0
    \end{bmatrix}
    -\omega^2
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \rho_{11}&\rho_{12}\\
        \rho_{12}&\rho_{22}
    \end{bmatrix}
    -i\omega
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0&0\\
        0&b_0
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right]=0.
    \label{eq:damped_dispersion}
\end{equation}
Thus the equilibrium value \(B_0\) already affects both linear compressional
waves.  This is the meaningful way to use the nonlinear finite-deformation
coefficient in a linear wave calculation: evaluate it at the prestressed
equilibrium state and use that tangent consistently in stress and storage.

At second order, the same linear system is forced.  The terms arising
explicitly from \(H_2\), \(B_J\), and \(B_p\) are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:second_order_system}
\begin{align}
    \rho_{11}u^{(2)}_{,tt}+\rho_{12}z^{(2)}_{,tt}
    -\partial_X\left(H e^{(2)}-B_0p^{(2)}\right)
    & =\partial_X
    \left[
        \frac{1}{2}H_2\left(e^{(1)}\right)^2
        -B_J e^{(1)}p^{(1)}
        -B_p\left(p^{(1)}\right)^2
    \right],
    \label{eq:second_order_total_momentum}\\
    \rho_{12}u^{(2)}_{,tt}+\rho_{22}z^{(2)}_{,tt}
    +b_0z^{(2)}_{,t}+p^{(2)}_{,X}
    & =0,
    \label{eq:second_order_relative_momentum}\\
    B_0e^{(2)}_{,t}+\frac{1}{M_0}p^{(2)}_{,t}
    +z^{(2)}_{,Xt}
    & =-\left(B_J-B_0\right)e^{(1)}e^{(1)}_{,t}
    -B_p\,p^{(1)}e^{(1)}_{,t}
    +\frac{p^{(1)}}{K_f}z^{(1)}_{,Xt}.
    \label{eq:second_order_storage}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The final term uses
\(\bar\rho_{f0}/\bar\rho_f=1-p^{(1)}/K_f+O(\varepsilon^2)\), where
\(K_f^{-1}=(\bar\rho_f^{-1}\partial\bar\rho_f/\partial\bar p_f)_0\).
If \(M\), inertia, or drag depend on state, their first derivatives add further
quadratic terms to the right-hand sides.  Equation
\eqref{eq:second_order_system} is therefore an explicit but deliberately
minimal second-order model.  It isolates the new \(B_J\) contribution without
mistaking it for the complete nonlinearity of a porous material.

A monochromatic first-order solution produces a zero-frequency correction and
forcing at \(2\omega\).  Since the operator on the left has two compressional
branches, the forcing can radiate onto either branch.  The generated amplitude
depends on phase mismatch, attenuation, and the projection of the source onto
the receiving mode.

\section{A modal nonlinear coefficient and an explicit second harmonic}

To connect \eqref{eq:second_order_system} to Hamilton-style nonlinear
acoustics, first consider a lossless, nondispersive frequency interval in which
one compressional mode is isolated.  Let
\begin{equation}
    \eta=\ln J,
    \qquad
    B_1=
    \left.
    \frac{\partial B}{\partial\eta}
    \right|_0
    =\left.
    J\frac{\partial B}{\partial J}
    \right|_0.
    \label{eq:biot_logarithmic_slope}
\end{equation}
The use of \(\eta\) is natural because the storage coupling in
\eqref{eq:finite_storage} is \(B\dot\eta\).  One thermodynamically closed local
specialization uses
\(\int_0^\eta B(s)\,\mathrm ds
=B_0\eta+B_1\eta^2/2+O(\eta^3)\) and takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{\mathrm{red}}(\eta,\zeta)
    =\frac{1}{2}H\eta^2+\frac{1}{6}H_2\eta^3
    +\frac{1}{2}M_0
    \left[
        \zeta-\int_0^\eta B(s)\,\mathrm ds
    \right]^2,
    \qquad
    \zeta=-z_{,X}.
    \label{eq:reduced_energy}
\end{equation}
It gives
\begin{equation}
    p=\frac{\partial\Psi_{\mathrm{red}}}{\partial\zeta},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial\Psi_{\mathrm{red}}}{\partial\eta}
    =H\eta+\frac{1}{2}H_2\eta^2-B(\eta)p.
    \label{eq:reduced_conjugates}
\end{equation}
Using the integral of \(B\), rather than the product \(B(\eta)\eta\), is what
makes the same state-dependent \(B\) appear in both storage and stress.

Let \(\mbf r_j=(r_{sj},r_{fj})^T\) be a right eigenvector of
\eqref{eq:compressional_eigenproblem}, normalized by
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{11}r_{sj}^2+2\rho_{12}r_{sj}r_{fj}
    +\rho_{22}r_{fj}^2=1.
    \label{eq:mode_normalization}
\end{equation}
In the reduced energy
\eqref{eq:reduced_energy}, the cubic coefficient projected onto mode \(j\) is
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_j
    =H_2r_{sj}^3
    +3M_0B_1r_{sj}^2
    \left(B_0r_{sj}+r_{fj}\right)
    +\gamma_j^{\mathrm{other}}.
    \label{eq:projected_cubic_coefficient}
\end{equation}
The second term is the explicit contribution from the nonlinear Biot
coefficient.  The term \(\gamma_j^{\mathrm{other}}\) collects the third-order
fluid equation of state, other skeleton invariants, finite-strain measure
conversion, and state-dependent inertia or drag omitted from
\eqref{eq:reduced_energy}.  Equation
\eqref{eq:projected_cubic_coefficient} also shows that the numerical value and
units of a modal coefficient depend on eigenvector and amplitude normalization.

If \(a_j(X,t)\) is the modal displacement, projection gives the leading
quadratic wave equation
\begin{equation}
    a_{j,tt}-c_j^2a_{j,XX}
    =\frac{\gamma_j}{2}
    \partial_X\left(a_{j,X}^2\right).
    \label{eq:projected_nonlinear_wave}
\end{equation}
For a right-going wave, let \(E_j=a_{j,X}\).  A one-way, weakly nonlinear
reduction of \eqref{eq:projected_nonlinear_wave} is
\begin{equation}
    E_{j,X}+\frac{1}{c_j}E_{j,t}
    =\kappa_j E_jE_{j,t},
    \qquad
    \kappa_j=\frac{\gamma_j}{2c_j^3}.
    \label{eq:modal_burgers}
\end{equation}
A narrow-band attenuation or dispersion operator may be added to the left-hand
side, but a constant thermoviscous coefficient is not generally adequate for
the slow Biot wave.

Equation \eqref{eq:modal_burgers} gives an immediate second-harmonic
prediction.  For
\begin{equation}
    E_j(0,t)=E_a\cos\omega t,
    \qquad
    \tau=t-\frac{X}{c_j},
    \label{eq:harmonic_boundary_data}
\end{equation}
successive approximation yields
\begin{equation}
    E_j(X,t)
    =E_a\cos\omega\tau
    -\frac{1}{2}\kappa_j\omega X E_a^2
    \sin 2\omega\tau
    +O\left(E_a^3\right).
    \label{eq:second_harmonic_prediction}
\end{equation}
Thus the initial second-harmonic-to-fundamental ratio grows as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{|E_{2\omega}|}{|E_\omega|}
    =\frac{1}{2}|\kappa_j|\omega X|E_a|,
    \label{eq:second_harmonic_ratio}
\end{equation}
before attenuation, phase mismatch, reflections, and pump depletion become
important.  The corresponding nominal shock distance is
\begin{equation}
    X_{\mathrm{shock}}
    =\frac{1}{|\kappa_j|\omega|E_a|}.
    \label{eq:shock_distance}
\end{equation}
This result provides an explicit second-harmonic prediction for a single,
weakly attenuated, nearly nondispersive mode.  In the general porous case,
\eqref{eq:second_harmonic_prediction} must be replaced by coupled modal
amplitude equations containing self-interaction coefficients
\(\kappa_j\), cross-mode coefficients, complex wavenumbers, and phase-mismatch
factors.

\section{Relation to classical nonlinear acoustics}

For a simple fluid, the second-order mass and momentum equations can be
combined into a scalar Westervelt equation, written schematically as
\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2p-\frac{1}{c_0^2}p_{,tt}
    =-\frac{\beta}{\rho_0c_0^4}
    \left(p^2\right)_{,tt}
    +\text{loss and diffraction terms},
    \qquad
    \beta=1+\frac{B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2A_{\mathrm{ac}}}.
    \label{eq:westervelt_equation}
\end{equation}
The subscripts ``ac'' distinguish the acoustic equation-of-state constants
\(A_{\mathrm{ac}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{ac}}\) from the poromechanical Biot
coefficient \(B\).  A one-way weak-loss reduction of
\eqref{eq:westervelt_equation} gives Burgers' equation
\cite{hamiltonblackstock}.

The porous reduction follows the same perturbation logic but not the same
single-field algebra.  One first solves the fast/slow eigenproblem, then
projects every quadratic source onto the selected left and right modes.  In the
simple-fluid limit only one longitudinal polarization survives, and a suitably
normalized \(\kappa_j\) reduces to the usual fluid nonlinearity.  In a porous
medium, different fast, slow, and shear interactions generally have different
coefficients and different attenuation.  Replacing \(c_0\) and \(\beta\) in a
fluid equation by measured porous values would miss that modal structure.

\section{A practical research program}

A defensible next study can be staged as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \textbf{Choose the equilibrium and frequency regime.}
    Specify porosity, confining stress, pore pressure, saturation, temperature,
    permeability, tortuosity model, and whether the slow mode is propagative or
    diffusion dominated.

    \item \textbf{Calibrate the linear problem.}
    Determine \(H\), \(M_0\), \(B_0\), the inertia matrix, and linear drag from
    independent measurements where possible.  Check both complex
    compressional branches using \eqref{eq:damped_dispersion} before fitting
    any nonlinear data.

    \item \textbf{Determine the quadratic coefficients.}
    Measure or model \(H_2\), \(B_1\), the fluid EOS derivatives, and the state
    derivatives of storage, inertia, and drag.  The definition
    \eqref{eq:nonlinear_biot_coefficient} provides a direct interpretation of
    \(B_1\) in terms of the curvature of intrinsic solid specific volume with
    skeleton deformation.

    \item \textbf{Project before reducing.}
    Compute the fast and slow eigenvectors, normalize them explicitly, and
    contract the full quadratic source with the corresponding adjoint modes.
    This produces self-interaction and cross-mode coefficients.  Only then
    decide whether a scalar equation such as \eqref{eq:modal_burgers} is
    justified.

    \item \textbf{Test harmonic generation and conversion.}
    Begin with a one-dimensional specimen and a single-frequency incident fast
    wave.  Compare fundamental attenuation, second-harmonic amplitude and
    phase, and any generated slow-mode response.  Repeat with confining stress
    and pore pressure varied independently; that variation is particularly
    useful for isolating \(B_1\) from contact nonlinearity in \(H_2\).
\end{enumerate}

The most useful first deliverable would be the lossless calculation through
\eqref{eq:projected_cubic_coefficient}, followed by the attenuating
second-harmonic boundary-value problem.  It is small enough to be checked
analytically, yet it directly tests whether the finite-deformation Biot
coefficient has a measurable effect on nonlinear wave propagation.

\section{Conclusion}

There is a sound extension of Hamilton-style second-order acoustics to a
fluid-saturated porous medium, but its natural starting point is a coupled Biot
system.  The first-order problem supplies fast and slow compressional
eigenvectors.  The second-order problem uses the same linear operator with
quadratic forcing from constitutive, geometric, inertial, storage, and drag
nonlinearities.  Modal projection then gives Burgers- or Westervelt-type
equations when a one-way single-mode approximation is valid.

The finite-deformation Biot coefficient contributes at two distinct levels.
Its equilibrium value \(B_0\) changes the linear wave speeds and
polarizations, while its state derivative \(B_1\) contributes to harmonic
generation and self-steepening.  Equations
\eqref{eq:projected_cubic_coefficient}--\eqref{eq:second_harmonic_prediction}
make that statement quantitative for a controlled one-dimensional
specialization.  The coefficient is only one part of the total nonlinearity,
but carrying it through the mode projection provides a direct route to
testable nonlinear wave predictions.
