\section{Correspondence to other theories}
\label{sec:correspondence_to_other_theories}

This section shows how the general equations reduce to models used in reservoir
simulation and electrochemical transport.  Each reduction states its
assumptions, identifies the closures that remain, and shows which variables and
balances become those of compositional flow, Nernst--Planck--Darcy transport,
black-oil flow, or Biot poromechanics.

\subsection{Classical compositional-flow limit and interpretation of the potentials}
\label{sec:classical_compositional_limit}

The averaged component balance reduces to the standard compositional-reservoir
balance after the fluid pore phases are isolated.  Write
$\phi_f=\phi S_f$ with $\sum_{f\in\mc F}S_f=1$, and use the averaged global
component balance \eqref{eq:averaged_global_component_balance}.  The result is
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(\phi\sum_{f\in\mc F}S_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\sum_{f\in\mc F}
	\left[
	\phi S_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha\mbf v_f
	+
	\mbf j_{f,{\rm disp}}^\alpha
	+
	\mbf j_{f,{\rm diff}}^\alpha
	\right]
	\right)
	=\sum_{f\in\mc F}\sum_m\nu_{f (m)}^\alpha\dot r_{(m)} .
	\label{eq:classical_total_mass_component_balance}
\end{equation}
%
This is the usual mass-based component balance per unit current mixture volume,
with mechanical dispersion and molecular diffusion retained explicitly.
Setting both relative fluxes to zero recovers the advective
component-balance structure of classical compositional reservoir simulation
\cite{balhoff2022multiphase}.  To keep the relative fluxes,
classical compositional simulation must supply the chemical potentials
algebraically; \eqref{eq:MC_admissible_conversion_component} evolves the
separate transfer potential \(\tau\), and
\eqref{eq:MC_phase_reference_transfer_work_recovery} supplies the remaining
phase-reference transfer-work levels.  Taking
\(\mbf v_s=\mbf 0\) in \eqref{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit} and using
$\bs\kappa_f=\kappa_{rf}\bs\kappa$ gives the Darcy law in the manuscript
notation,
%
\begin{equation}
	\phi S_f\mbf v_f
	=
	-\frac{\bs\kappa\,\kappa_{rf}}{\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}}
	\left(
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g
	\right),
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:classical_fixed_frame_darcy_velocity}
\end{equation}
%
The same history state may enter \(\kappa_{rf}\) when relative-permeability
hysteresis is retained.

A concrete classical closure set is
%
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mbf v_s &= \mbf 0, \\
		\bar p_f &= \bar p_{\rm ref}+p_{c,f}(\{S_g\}_{g\in\mc F},\mbf h,\theta_{\mc F}), \\
		\bar\rho_f &= \bar\rho_f(\bar p_{\rm ref},\theta_{\mc F},\{\eta_f^\alpha\}), \qquad
			\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}
			=
			\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}(\bar p_{\rm ref},\theta_{\mc F},\{\eta_f^\alpha\}), \\
		\mu_f^\alpha
		&=
		\mc G_f
		+
		\frac{\partial \mc G_f}{\partial\eta_f^\alpha}
		-
		\sum_\beta
		\eta_f^\beta
		\frac{\partial \mc G_f}{\partial\eta_f^\beta},
		\\
			\mbf j_{f,{\rm disp}}^\alpha+\mbf j_{f,{\rm diff}}^\alpha
			&=
			-\sum_{\beta\ne N}
		\left(
		\mbf D_{f,{\rm disp}}^{\alpha\beta}
		+
			\mbf D_{f,{\rm diff}}^{\alpha\beta}
			\right)
			\theta_{\mc F}
			\nabla_{\mbf x}
			\left(
			\frac{\mu_f^\beta-\mu_f^N}{\theta_{\mc F}}
			\right),
		\qquad \alpha\ne N,
		\\
		\mbf j_{f,k}^N
		&=
		-\sum_{\alpha\ne N}
		\mbf j_{f,k}^\alpha,
		\qquad
		k\in\{{\rm disp},{\rm diff}\},
		\\
		\mu_f^\alpha &= \mu_g^\alpha
		\quad \text{for equilibrated fluid phases } f,g,
		\qquad
		f,g\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:classical_compositional_closure_set}
\end{equation}
%
Here \(\mc G_f\) is the specific Gibbs energy supplied by the EOS or flash model.
Although \(\mc G_f\) is physically evaluated for mass fractions whose sum is one,
the partial derivatives in
\eqref{eq:classical_compositional_closure_set} may be taken from any
differentiable extension to nearby independent compositions.  The displayed
combination is independent of that choice and gives the absolute chemical
potential.  The same expression follows by differentiating the total phase
Gibbs energy with respect to the mass of component \(\alpha\) at fixed
pressure, temperature, and masses of the other components.  Choose component
\(N\) as the reference, set
\(\eta_f^N=1-\sum_{\gamma\ne N}\eta_f^\gamma\), and use the \(N-1\)
chemical-potential differences.  These differences describe every independent
composition change that preserves the mass-fraction sum.  The final flux row
makes each flux family zero-sum, so the reduced mobility matrices act on those
\(N-1\) independent changes.  The transport row retains the full
nonisothermal force from \eqref{eq:MC_relative_transport_closures}.  When
\(\theta_{\mc F}\) is spatially uniform, it reduces to the
chemical-potential-difference gradient used below.

In an isothermal composition-diffusion specialization at fixed phase pressure,
eliminate \(\eta_f^N\) and differentiate the \(N-1\) chemical-potential
differences with respect to the independent mass fractions:
%
\begin{equation}
	\nabla_{\mbf x}
	\left(
	\mu_f^\beta-\mu_f^N
	\right)
	=
	\sum_{\gamma\ne N}
	\frac{
		\partial
		\left(
		\mu_f^\beta-\mu_f^N
		\right)
	}{
		\partial\eta_f^\gamma
	}
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\eta_f^\gamma ,
	\qquad
	\beta\ne N,\quad f\in\mc F,
	\label{eq:composition_gradient_chemical_potential_tangent}
\end{equation}
%
where the derivatives are evaluated after setting
\(\eta_f^N=1-\sum_{\gamma\ne N}\eta_f^\gamma\).  Substitution converts the
admissible potential-gradient flux into the familiar composition-gradient
diffusion and dispersion law.  For the \(N-1\) independent component balances,
define the effective Fickian diffusion--dispersion tensor by
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\mbf D}_{f}^{\alpha\gamma}
	=
	\sum_{\beta\ne N}
	\left(
	\mbf D_{f,{\rm disp}}^{\alpha\beta}
	+
	\mbf D_{f,{\rm diff}}^{\alpha\beta}
	\right)
	\frac{
		\partial
		\left(
		\mu_f^\beta-\mu_f^N
		\right)
	}{
		\partial\eta_f^\gamma
	},
	\qquad
	\alpha,\gamma\ne N,\quad f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:effective_fickian_diffusion_dispersion_tensor}
\end{equation}
%
Substituting these closures into
\eqref{eq:classical_total_mass_component_balance} gives
%
\begin{multline}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(\phi\sum_{f\in\mc F}S_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha\right)
	\\
	\quad
	+
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left[
	\sum_{f\in\mc F}
	\left(
	\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha
	\phi S_f\mbf v_f
	-
			\sum_{\gamma\ne N}
			\bar{\mbf D}_{f}^{\alpha\gamma}
			\nabla_{\mbf x}\eta_f^\gamma
	\right)
		\right]
		=
		\sum_{f\in\mc F}\sum_m\nu_{f (m)}^\alpha\dot r_{(m)},
		\qquad
		\alpha\ne N.
	\label{eq:classical_compositional_fickian_balance}
\end{multline}
%
This is the standard reservoir compositional-flow form: storage is written in
terms of porosity, saturation, phase density, and phase composition; transport is
the phase Darcy flux multiplied by the component mass fraction; capillary
pressure and its capillary-history state reconstruct phase pressures from the
chosen reference pressure
\(\bar p_{\rm ref}\); and the EOS or flash closure supplies densities,
viscosities, equilibrium compositions, and the chemical-potential/composition
tangent that converts the entropy-admissible \(D\nabla\mu\) flux into the usual
Fickian composition-gradient form.  The reference-component balance follows
from the summed mass balance, the \(N-1\) displayed component balances, the
zero-sum relative-flux condition, and mechanism mass conservation; it is not an
additional independent equation.
The phase-labelled stoichiometric coefficients also represent a pure phase
transformation as the special case in which the component label is unchanged and
the two nonzero entries satisfy $\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha=-1$ and
$\nu_{\zeta (m)}^\alpha=+1$; after summing over phases, its contribution to the
total component source vanishes, but it still changes the phase saturations and
phase compositions.

Capillary pressure enters the compositional-flow limit through the interfacial
energy.  The general theory defines saturations through
\eqref{eq:fluid_saturation_definitions}, assigns an interfacial Helmholtz energy
\eqref{eq:interfacial_energy_density}, and recovers phase pressure differences in
\eqref{eq:wetting_nonwetting_capillary_pressure_recovery}.  At fixed
\(\mbf h\), capillary, or electrocapillary, laws are algebraic
pressure-difference closures.  The evolution laws constrained by
\eqref{eq:MC_capillary_history_admissibility} select the drainage, imbibition,
and scanning path.  A neutral
compositional implementation
typically chooses one reference pressure and reconstructs the other phase
pressures from capillary-pressure relations before evaluating the Darcy fluxes.
In this variational model, this is the algebraic specialization of the
more general statement \(\bar p_f=\bar p_E+\gamma_f+\omega_f^+\).  When
electrical enthalpy is retained, the pressure reconstruction includes the
correction \(\omega_n^+-\omega_w^+\) in
\eqref{eq:wetting_nonwetting_capillary_pressure_recovery}; in the neutral limit
this reduces to the usual capillary-pressure closure.  The offsets redistribute
the phase pressures and combine with the Maxwell-stress and phase-volume
electrical forces according to
\eqref{eq:MC_fluid_pressure_gradient_rollup}.

The usual within-phase composition constraint is also recovered:
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1 .
	\label{eq:classical_composition_constraints}
\end{equation}
%
A conventional compositional simulator may supply the equation of state,
phase-equilibrium rules, capillary relations, component partitioning, and
stress law as separate inputs.  Here the phase Helmholtz energies and the
interfacial and electrical potentials supply the reversible quantities:
pressure, entropy, stress, and the composition and storage potentials.
Permeability, diffusivity, mobility, and reaction-rate laws remain constitutive
closures, but the same stored potentials supply the forces that drive them.

Classical flash calculations impose phase-equilibrium closures, commonly written
as fugacity equalities.  In the manuscript notation, the reversible transfer of
component $\alpha$ from phase $\xi$ to phase $\zeta$ first gives equality of
the generalized transfer potentials,
%
\begin{equation}
	\left(
	\psi_\zeta+L_\zeta^\alpha+z_\zeta^\alpha\varphi
	\right)
	-
	\left(
	\psi_\xi+L_\xi^\alpha+z_\xi^\alpha\varphi
	\right)
	=0 .
	\label{eq:classical_phase_equilibrium_mu}
\end{equation}
%
At full reversible equilibrium, the active phases participating in the
equilibrium calculation have no relative motion, so
\(\mbf v_\xi=\mbf v_{\mc S}\) and
\(D_\xi\tau/Dt=D_{\mc S}\tau/Dt\).  The reference normalization
\eqref{eq:MC_transfer_work_reference_normalization}, the Euler identity
\eqref{eq:MC_neutral_component_euler_identity}, and the reference evolution
\eqref{eq:MC_admissible_conversion_component} give
\(D_{\mc S}\tau/Dt
=\mbf v_{\mc S}\cdot\mbf v_{\mc S}/2\).  Hence the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:MC_generalized_transfer_phase_offset} vanishes in every equilibrated
phase, and
\(\psi_\xi+L_\xi^\alpha+z_\xi^\alpha\varphi=\mu_\xi^\alpha\).
Equation \eqref{eq:classical_phase_equilibrium_mu} then becomes the
electrochemical-potential equality for a charged component.  For a neutral
component, or when the same specific charge is transferred between phases at
the same electric potential, the electrical contribution cancels and this
reduces to the chemical-potential, or fugacity-equality, condition used in
isothermal flash calculations.  Away from phase equilibrium, only the
within-phase differences are fixed to electrochemical-potential differences by
\eqref{eq:MC_generalized_transfer_potential_difference}; the \(P-1\)
phase-reference levels are recovered from
\eqref{eq:MC_phase_reference_transfer_work_recovery}.  The single
first-order equation \eqref{eq:MC_admissible_conversion_component} evolves the
Bedford--Drumheller transfer potential \(\tau\) from its prescribed initial
datum.

The traditional affinity remains the stoichiometric sum of the
electrochemical potentials,
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_{(m)}
	=
	-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\mu_\xi^\alpha
	\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha .
	\label{eq:classical_affinity_projection}
\end{equation}
%
At full thermodynamic equilibrium, the temperatures are equal, the
no-relative-motion argument above identifies every generalized transfer
potential with its electrochemical potential, and imposing
\(\mc A_{(m)}=0\) gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha=0,
	\label{eq:classical_equilibrium_affinity}
\end{equation}
%
which is the classical statement that the stoichiometric sum of chemical
potentials vanishes.  Away from equilibrium, the generalized conversion power
differs from this traditional affinity by the explicit phase-transfer work in
\eqref{eq:MC_generalized_conversion_affinity_relation}.  That contribution
vanishes for chemistry that conserves mass separately in every phase and
remains for phase change, precipitation, dissolution, and pore filling.  Away
from local thermal equilibrium, every uncoupled fluid--solid mechanism is
driven by the gauge-invariant neutral temperature-weighted stoichiometric sum
in \eqref{eq:MC_onsager_reaction_rate}.  Charged transfer between thermal
subsystems may couple that reaction rate to reaction-energy transfer subject
to \eqref{eq:MC_reaction_energy_coupled_admissibility}.  When
\(\theta_{\mc F}=\theta_{\mc S}=\theta\) and the transfer-work contribution
vanishes, the uncoupled law reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
	\dot r_{(m)}
	=
	\frac{K_{(m)}}{\theta}\mc A_{(m)},
	\qquad \mc A_{(m)}\dot r_{(m)}\ge 0 .
	\label{eq:classical_kinetic_affinity_closure}
\end{equation}
%
Here \(K_{(m)}/\theta\) is the conventional linear affinity mobility.  The
temperature-weighted law therefore recovers the classical isothermal sign
convention for phasewise mass-conserving chemistry without assigning a separate
temperature to a cross-subsystem mechanism.  General phase-changing mechanisms
retain the additional term shown in
\eqref{eq:MC_generalized_conversion_affinity_relation}.

The momentum equations contain the material-insertion contribution
%
	\begin{equation}
		\sum_\alpha\sum_\xi\phasedot{c}_\xi^\alpha
		\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\tau-\mbf v_\xi\right).
		\label{eq:conversion_momentum_interpretation}
	\end{equation}
%
Here \(\phasedot{c}_\xi^\alpha\) is the full component material-addition rate;
\eqref{eq:reference_conversion_accumulation_rate} separates conversion from
transport relative to the phase.  Diffusion and dispersion are driven by the gauge-invariant
nonisothermal combination of neutral-potential and electric forces and closed
by the force equations
\eqref{eq:MC_onsager_dispersion_stationarity} and
\eqref{eq:MC_onsager_diffusion_stationarity}.  In a Darcy reduction, these
contributions are incorporated into the phase-flux closure.

The remaining multipliers enforce familiar constraints.  The multiplier
\(\lambda\) fills the available volume with the represented phases,
\(\Pi_\xi\) makes the mass fractions sum to one, and \(\pi_\xi^\alpha\)
enforces the component storage relation.  The transfer multiplier \(\tau\) is
the Bedford--Drumheller kinematic potential associated with accumulated
material insertion.  The generalized coefficients
\(\psi_\xi+L_\xi^\alpha+z_\xi^\alpha\varphi\) appear in the variational
conversion power, whereas the traditional affinity is the stoichiometric sum
of \(\mu_\xi^\alpha\).  Relative-transport closures use the
gauge-invariant nonisothermal split of the neutral-potential difference and
the electric field.  Conversion contributes to phase momentum through
\(\nabla_{\mbf x}\tau\).  Diffusion and dispersion enter the same material
measure, but their rates are closed by chemical and electric driving forces.
Varying the phase Jacobian on phase-attached regions produces the
material-insertion momentum term.

\subsection{Black-oil limit}
\label{sec:black_oil_limit}

The black-oil model is obtained by further specializing
\eqref{eq:classical_total_mass_component_balance}.  Take the fluid phases to be
water, oil, and gas, \(\mc F=\{w,o,g\}\), neglect mechanical dispersion and
molecular diffusion by setting
\(\mbf j_{f,{\rm disp}}^\alpha=\mbf j_{f,{\rm diff}}^\alpha=\mbf 0\), use a
fixed solid frame, and impose the neutral nonelectrical specialization
	\(\omega_\xi^+=0\), \(z_\xi^\alpha=0\), and \(\mbf E=\mbf d=\mbf0\).
	Replace the full
component EOS by black-oil formation-volume factors and solution-gas data.  In
stock-tank units the phase/component storage factors are identified as
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar\rho_w\eta_w^W=\frac{\rho_{w,{\rm sc}}}{B_w},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_o\eta_o^O=\frac{\rho_{o,{\rm sc}}}{B_o},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_g\eta_g^G=\frac{\rho_{g,{\rm sc}}}{B_g},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_o\eta_o^G=\frac{\rho_{g,{\rm sc}}R_s}{B_o}.
	\label{eq:black_oil_storage_identifications}
\end{equation}
%
Here \(W\), \(O\), and \(G\) label stock-tank water, oil, and gas components;
\(B_w\), \(B_o\), and \(B_g\) are formation-volume factors; \(R_s\) is the
dissolved gas-oil ratio, and the subscript \({\rm sc}\) denotes surface
conditions.  Let \(\mbf q_f=\phi S_f\mbf v_f\) be the phase volumetric flux and
divide the water, oil-component, and gas-component balances by their
corresponding surface densities to obtain
%
\begin{align}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi\frac{S_w}{B_w}\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\frac{\mbf q_w}{B_w}\right)
	 & = 0,
	\label{eq:black_oil_water_balance} \\
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi\frac{S_o}{B_o}\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\frac{\mbf q_o}{B_o}\right)
	 & = 0,
	\label{eq:black_oil_oil_balance}   \\
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left[
		\phi\left(\frac{S_g}{B_g}+R_s\frac{S_o}{B_o}\right)
		\right]
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left[
		\frac{\mbf q_g}{B_g}
			+R_s\frac{\mbf q_o}{B_o}
		\right]
	 & = 0 .
	\label{eq:black_oil_gas_balance}
\end{align}
%
These are the standard black-oil conservation laws: water occupies the
water phase, the stock-tank oil component occupies the oil phase, and the
stock-tank gas component is present in the gas phase and dissolved in the oil
phase through \(R_s\).  Black-oil PVT closures \(B_f\) and \(R_s\) determine
the phase/component storage factors.

The phase fluxes follow from the neutral, conversion-free,
negligible-inertia reduction \eqref{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit}.  With
\(\bs\kappa_f=k_{rf}\bs\kappa\), where $k_{rf}$ is the relative permeability
and may depend on \(\mbf h\),
the fixed-frame flux closure is
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf q_f
	=
	-\frac{k_{rf}}{\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}}\bs\kappa
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g\right),
	\qquad
	f\in\{w,o,g\}.
	\label{eq:black_oil_darcy_flux}
\end{equation}
%
If oil pressure is chosen as the black-oil reference pressure, the general
capillary-pressure closure in \eqref{eq:wetting_nonwetting_capillary_pressure_recovery}
specializes to the usual water-oil and
gas-oil relations
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar p_w=\bar p_o-p_{cow}(S_w,\mbf h),
	\qquad
	\bar p_g=\bar p_o+p_{cgo}(S_g,\mbf h).
	\label{eq:black_oil_capillary_pressure_closures}
\end{equation}
%
These closures are then used with one pressure, for example \(\bar p_o\), and
two independent saturations because \(S_w+S_o+S_g=1\).  The remaining phase
pressures are reconstructed from \eqref{eq:black_oil_capillary_pressure_closures}
before evaluating \eqref{eq:black_oil_darcy_flux}.  Pair-specific entries of
\(\mbf h\) store reversal and trapping data for the water--oil and gas--oil
paths.  Holding \(\mbf h\) fixed recovers single-valued saturation functions;
updating it with an admissible loading--unloading rule gives drainage,
imbibition, and scanning curves for capillary pressure and, when selected,
relative permeability.  In a potential-based three-phase specialization, both
pairwise pressure laws must be derivatives of the common \(\gamma\), which
imposes the corresponding cross-derivative compatibility.  Independently fitted
pairwise laws are an empirical reservoir closure when that condition is not
satisfied.

\subsection{Nernst--Planck--Darcy limit}
\label{sec:nernst_planck_darcy_limit}

The electrostatic single-fluid specialization recovers the
Nernst--Planck--Darcy system.  Take one isothermal fluid phase in a fixed solid
	frame, set the reaction and phase-transformation rates and mechanical-dispersion
	fluxes to zero, and neglect inertia, gravity, and capillarity.  Let \(\phi_f\)
be constant, take a linear isotropic dielectric response
\(\mbf d_f=\epsilon_f\mbf E\) with constant \(\epsilon_f\), set fixed solid
charge and the solid electrical displacement to zero, and assign all free
charge to the fluid components.  A background solid dielectric contribution
could instead be folded into a specified effective \(\epsilon_f\), but is not
retained independently in this limit.  Gauss' law then gives
\(\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot(\phi_f\mbf d_f)
=\sum_\alpha z_f^\alpha\rho_f^\alpha\).  Because
\(\nabla_{\mbf x}\times\mbf E=\mbf0\), the electrical and pressure forces in
\eqref{eq:generalized_fluid_darcy_mass_flux} reduce according to
%
\begin{align}
	&-\phi_f\nabla_{\mbf x}
	\left(
		\bar p_E+\gamma_f
	\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
		\phi_f\mbf E\otimes\mbf d_f
	\right)
	\notag \\
	&\qquad =
	-\phi_f\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f
	+
	\left(
		\sum_\alpha z_f^\alpha\rho_f^\alpha
	\right)\mbf E,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_maxwell_force_reduction}
\end{align}
%
Here the pressure relation
\(\bar p_f=\bar p_E+\gamma_f+\omega_f^+\), with
\(\omega_f^+=-\frac{1}{2}\mbf E\cdot\mbf d_f\), absorbs the isotropic part of
the Maxwell stress.  Thus the tensor-divergence term in the general flux law
becomes the familiar electric body force exerted on the mobile charge.

With \(\mbf v_s=\mbf0\), the reaction-free form of
\eqref{eq:modified_relative_flux_permeability}, the component balances,
\eqref{eq:MC_relative_transport_closures}, and Gauss' law give
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_limit}
	\begin{align}
		\phi_f\mbf v_f
		&=
		-\frac{\bs\kappa_f}{\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}}
		\left[
			\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f
			-
			\frac{1}{\phi_f}
			\left(
				\sum_\alpha z_f^\alpha\rho_f^\alpha
			\right)\mbf E
		\right],
		\qquad
		f\in\mc F,
		\label{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_velocity}
		\\
		\frac{\partial\rho_f^\alpha}{\partial t}
		&+
		\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left[
			\rho_f^\alpha\mbf v_f
			-
			\sum_{\beta\ne N}
			\mbf D_{f,{\rm diff}}^{\alpha\beta}
			\nabla_{\mbf x}
			\left(
				\hat\mu_f^\beta-\hat\mu_f^N
				+
				\left(
				z_f^\beta-z_f^N
				\right)\varphi
			\right)
		\right]
		=0,
		\qquad
		\alpha\ne N,\quad f\in\mc F,
		\label{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_component_balance}
		\\
		\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left(
			\phi_f\mbf d_f
		\right)
		&=
		\sum_\alpha z_f^\alpha\rho_f^\alpha,
		\qquad
		\mbf E=-\nabla_{\mbf x}\varphi,
		\qquad
		f\in\mc F .
		\label{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_gauss_law}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
The second equation is the thermodynamic Nernst--Planck law: an ideal-dilute
choice of \(\hat\mu_f^\alpha\) separates its force into concentration-diffusion
and electromigration terms.  The reference-component flux is reconstructed by
\eqref{eq:MC_relative_transport_closures}, and its balance follows from the
total mass balance and the \(N-1\) displayed component balances.  If the
coefficients are nondimensionalized,
\(\phi_f=1\), and the two ionic components have opposite valences and equal
diagonal diffusivities, \eqref{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_limit} has the standard
form studied by Ignatova and Shu
\cite[eqs.~(1.1)--(1.5)]{ignatova2021charged_fluids}.
Ion exchange extends this fluid-only limit by retaining the charged solid-site
components described in \sref{conservation_of_charge}.  Their fixed charge then
contributes to the mixture Gauss law, while the mobile fluid charge in
\eqref{eq:nernst_planck_darcy_velocity} supplies the electrical fluid-driving
force; exchange kinetics or equilibrium relations close the solid--fluid
component transfer.

\subsection{Single-solid, single-fluid Biot limit}
\label{sec:single_solid_single_fluid_biot_limit}

The classical Biot poromechanics specialization has one solid skeleton phase
and one fluid pore phase, \(\mc S=\{s\}\) and \(\mc F=\{f\}\), with
\(\dot r_{(m)}=0\), \(\omega_\xi^+=0\), \(\mbf d=\mbf0\), and
\(\gamma=0\) because no fluid--fluid interface remains.  The equivalent
pressure relation \eqref{eq:equivalent_pore_pressure_capillary_relation} then
gives the unique fluid pressure,
\(\bar p_E=\bar p_f\).  The solid pressure relation
\eqref{eq:MC_phase_equivalent_pressure} also gives
\(\bar p_s=\bar p_E\).  In this limit
\eqref{eq:MC_solid_biot_rollup_coefficient} reduces to \(B=B_s\), so the
nonlinear coefficient in \eqref{eq:MC_phase_biot_coefficient} is the
Biot coefficient of the single skeleton.

Under \(\dot r_{(m)}=0\), the overall momentum balance
\eqref{eq:MC_overall_momentum_nonlinear_biot} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{\xi\in\{s,f\}}\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	=
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
	-B\bar p_f\mbf I
	\right)
	+\rho\mbf g,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_spatial_momentum}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the total Cauchy stress is the fixed-pressure skeleton stress minus the
Biot pressure correction.  For a quasi-static skeleton solve the inertial term
is dropped, giving the usual equilibrium equation
%
\begin{equation}
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
	-B\bar p_f\mbf I
	\right)
	+\rho\mbf g=\mbf 0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_quasistatic_momentum}
\end{equation}
%
The corresponding Biot effective first Piola--Kirchhoff stress is the
single-solid specialization of the general definition
\eqref{eq:solid_reference_effective_piola_stress}.
%
Multiplying \eqref{eq:biot_limit_quasistatic_momentum} by \(J\) and applying
the Piola identity gives the solid-reference equilibrium equation
%
\begin{equation}
	\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot
	\left(
	\mbf P^{\prime\prime}
	-B\bar p_f J\mbf F^{-T}
	\right)
	+J\rho\mbf g
	=\mbf 0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_reference_momentum}
\end{equation}
%
The fluid transport equation is the single-fluid specialization of the
solid-reference component balance.  Let
\(\mbf w_f=\phi_f\bar\rho_f\left(\mbf v_f-\mbf v_s\right)\) be the current
relative mass flux and let \(\mbf W_f\) be its Piola transform defined in
\eqref{eq:reference_relative_mass_flux}.  In
\eqref{eq:solid_reference_fluid_component_balance}, take the single component in
the single fluid phase to be the fluid itself.  Then \(\eta_f^f=1\), and the
conversion source vanishes.  The zero-sum condition
\eqref{eq:averaged_relative_flux_sum} also makes the single component's
diffusion and dispersion fluxes vanish, and the component storage
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^f\) reduces to the Lagrangian fluid mass
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).  The corresponding current-balance form is obtained from
\eqref{eq:averaged_component_spatial_mass} by the same specialization and by
splitting the fluid velocity into solid motion plus relative flux,
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\mbf v_s\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_current_fluid_mass_balance}
\end{equation}
%
Using the material derivative following the solid skeleton,
\(D_s/Dt=\partial/\partial t+\mbf v_s\cdot\nabla_{\mbf x}\), this
becomes
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{D_s}{Dt}\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\phi_f\bar\rho_f\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_solid_derivative_fluid_mass_balance}
\end{equation}
%
Since \({D_sJ}/{Dt}=J\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s\), the first two terms
combine into the derivative of \(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\),
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{J}\frac{D_s}{Dt}
	\left(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_jacobian_current_mass_balance}
\end{equation}
%
Multiplying by \(J\), using the divergence transform
\eqref{eq:solid_reference_flux_divergence}, and observing that
\(D_s/Dt=\partial/\partial t\) at fixed \(\mbf X\) gives
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass},
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the finite-deformation storage variable is the Lagrangian fluid mass
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).  Equivalently, with \(\Phi_f=J\phi_f\),
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\Phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_lagrangian_pore_volume_mass_balance}
\end{equation}
%
If the fluid is barotropic, \(\bar\rho_f=\bar\rho_f\left(\bar p_f\right)\), and
\({\rm d}\bar\rho_f/{\rm d}\bar p_f=\bar\rho_f/K_f\), where \(K_f\) is the
fluid bulk modulus, then
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass} can be written as
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar\rho_f
	\left[
		\frac{\partial \Phi_f}{\partial t}
		+\frac{\Phi_f}{K_f}
		\frac{\partial \bar p_f}{\partial t}
	\right]
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_barotropic_storage_balance}
\end{equation}
%
For comparison with the classical Biot storage equation, we supplement the
phase energy with a finite-deformation extension of the standard small-strain
porosity tangent summarized by
\cite{coussy2004poromechanics,detournay1993fundamentals}.  In this extension,
\(B\) plays the role of the usual Biot coefficient \(\alpha\).  We also assume
homogeneous solid grains, so the unjacketed moduli coincide and uniform
unjacketed loading does not change porosity.  Using the same \(B\) in the stress and pore-volume
tangents is an additional reciprocity and storage assumption.  Over a finite
loading history, this tangent must come from an integrable storage law or serve
as the evolution law for \(\Phi_f\); an arbitrary state-dependent \(B\) need
not produce a path-independent \(\Phi_f(J,\bar p_f)\).  With \(B\) fixed over
the increment, \(K_s\) denoting the solid-grain bulk modulus, and \(M\)
denoting the Biot storage modulus,
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial\Phi_f}{\partial t}
	=
	B\frac{1}{J}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}
	+\frac{B-\Phi_f}{K_s}
	\frac{\partial\bar p_f}{\partial t},
	\qquad
	\frac{1}{M}
	=
	\frac{B-\Phi_f}{K_s}
	+\frac{\Phi_f}{K_f},
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_porosity_tangent_storage}
\end{equation}
%
Stable pressure storage additionally requires \(1/M>0\).
Substitution gives the finite-deformation pressure-storage balance
%
\begin{equation}
	B\frac{1}{J}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}
	+\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial\bar p_f}{\partial t}
	+\frac{1}{\bar\rho_f}\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_pressure_storage_balance}
\end{equation}
%
Finally, take
\(J\simeq 1+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u\) and
\(\mbf F\simeq\mbf I\), and neglect spatial density gradients in the
mass-to-volume flux conversion while retaining temporal barotropic
compressibility in \(1/M\).  With the current volumetric relative flux
\(\mbf q_f=\mbf w_f/\bar\rho_f\), the exact Piola identity and this
small-strain, small-compressibility transport approximation give
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{\bar\rho_f}
	\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=
	J\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf q_f
	+
	J\mbf q_f\cdot
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\ln\bar\rho_f
	\simeq
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf q_f,
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_mass_to_volume_flux_approximation}
\end{equation}
%
The fluid--skeleton interaction closure gives, in the current configuration,
%
\begin{equation}
	\phi_f\left(\mbf v_f-\mbf v_s\right)
	=
	-\frac{\bs\kappa_f}{\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}}
	\left(
		\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f
		-\bar\rho_f\mbf g
	\right),
	\qquad
	f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_darcy_law}
\end{equation}
%
Equations \eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_momentum} and
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_pressure_storage_balance} are the finite-deformation
momentum and fluid-storage equations of the single-fluid Biot specialization.
The pressure-driven Darcy law \eqref{eq:biot_limit_darcy_law} closes the
relative fluid transport without an additional Helmholtz-gradient restriction.

The familiar small-strain Biot equations follow by linearizing these
solid-reference equations about \(\mbf F=\mbf I\) and \(J=1\), using a linear
elastic law for \(\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}\), and substituting the Darcy flux
directly into the fluid balance.  In this linearization,
\(B\), \(M\), \(\bs\kappa_f\), \(\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}\), and \(\bar\rho_f\) are frozen at
the reference state, and \(\Phi_{f0}=\phi_{f0}\) when \(J_0=1\).  The
source-free reduced system is
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:linear_biot_limit}
	\begin{align}
		\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left(
		\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
		-B\bar p_f\mbf I
		\right)
		+\rho\mbf g
		 & =\mbf 0,
		\qquad
		f\in\mc F,\quad s\in\mc S,
		\label{eq:linear_biot_momentum} \\
		\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial \bar p_f}{\partial t}
		+B\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
		\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u\right)
		-\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left[
			\frac{\bs\kappa_f}{\mu_f^{\mathrm{visc}}}
			\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g\right)
			\right]
		 & =0,
		\qquad
		f\in\mc F .
		\label{eq:linear_biot_fluid_content}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
Here \(\mbf u\) is the small displacement of the skeleton.  The pressure
coupling in \eqref{eq:linear_biot_momentum} comes from the same fixed-pressure
Legendre stress split that produced \eqref{eq:MC_phase_biot_split}; the
fluid-content coupling in \eqref{eq:linear_biot_fluid_content} is the
small-strain form of the finite-deformation storage \(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).
The coefficient limits are explicit in this frozen linear system:
\(K_s\to\infty\) removes solid-grain compressibility from \(1/M\),
\(K_f\to\infty\) removes fluid compressibility, and taking both moduli to
infinity makes pressure a constraint with \(1/M\to0\).  A rigid skeleton
removes the \(B\,\partial_t(\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u)\) term and leaves the
pressure-diffusion equation, whereas zero relative flux gives the undrained
storage relation
\((1/M)\,\partial_t\bar p_f+
B\,\partial_t(\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u)=0\).
The classical Biot system is therefore the single-solid, single-fluid,
no-conversion, linearized limit of the variational mixture model.
