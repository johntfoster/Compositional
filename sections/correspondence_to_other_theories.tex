\section{Correspondence to other theories}
\label{sec:correspondence_to_other_theories}

\subsection{Classical compositional-flow limit and interpretation of the multipliers}
\label{sec:classical_compositional_limit}

The preceding equations contain the usual compositional-reservoir structure as a
special limit.  Restrict attention to the fluid pore phases, write
$\phi_f=\phi S_f$ with $\sum_{f\in\mc F}S_f=1$, and use the phase-resolved
source representation \eqref{eq:unified_stoich_current} in the global component
balance \eqref{eq:spatial_mass_2}.  The result is
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(\phi\sum_{f\in\mc F}S_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\sum_{f\in\mc F}
	\phi S_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^\alpha\mbf v_f
	\right)
	=\sum_{f\in\mc F}\sum_m\nu_{f (m)}^\alpha\dot r_{(m)} .
	\label{eq:classical_total_mass_component_balance}
\end{equation}
%
This is the usual phase/component compositional balance, written per unit mass
rather than per mole and with mechanical dispersion and molecular diffusion
omitted.  It is the mass counterpart of Balhoff's standard compositional balance
\cite[eq.~(7.15b)]{balhoff2022multiphase}.  Taking $\mbf v_s=\mbf 0$ in
\eqref{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit} and using
$\bs\kappa_f=\kappa_{fr}(\{S_g\}_{g\in\mc F})\bs\kappa$ gives Darcy
law in the present notation.  The
current balance law already has the standard compositional-flow form once the
reservoir variables $\phi$ and $S_f$ are introduced.  The difference is that the
present stoichiometric coefficients retain the phase label.  A pure phase
transformation is the special case in which the component label is unchanged and
the two nonzero entries satisfy $\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha=-1$ and
$\nu_{\zeta (m)}^\alpha=+1$; after summing over phases, its contribution to the
total component source vanishes, but it still changes the phase saturations and
phase compositions.

Capillary pressure enters the compositional-flow limit through the interfacial
energy, not as an independent balance law.  The general theory defines
saturations through \eqref{eq:fluid_saturation_definitions}, assigns an
interfacial Helmholtz energy \eqref{eq:interfacial_energy_density}, and recovers
phase pressure differences in
\eqref{eq:wetting_nonwetting_capillary_pressure_recovery}.  Thus capillary laws
are algebraic pressure-difference closures.  A compositional implementation
typically chooses one reference pressure and reconstructs the other phase
pressures from capillary-pressure relations before evaluating the Darcy fluxes.
In the present variational model this is the algebraic specialization of the
more general statement \(\bar p_f=\bar p_E+\gamma_f\): capillary offsets
redistribute the phase pressures while the saturation-weighted pressure gradient
reduces to the equivalent-pressure gradient in
\eqref{eq:MC_fluid_pressure_gradient_rollup}.

The remaining algebraic structure is also recovered.  The within-phase
composition constraints are
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1 ,
	\label{eq:classical_composition_constraints}
\end{equation}
%
The thermodynamic distinction appears in how the phase/component potentials are
obtained.  A traditional compositional simulator supplies an equation of state,
phase-equilibrium rules, capillary-pressure relations, component partitioning,
and stress laws as separate closure ingredients.  In the present formulation the
reversible thermodynamic part is instead generated from the Helmholtz
free-energy structure.  Thus pressure, entropy, stress, composition derivatives,
and normalized
component storage potentials are not independent reversible closures; they are
conjugate derivatives or multipliers associated with the constrained Helmholtz
structure.  Transport and reaction kinetics still require dissipative closures,
such as mobilities, permeabilities, diffusivities, and reaction-rate laws, but
the thermodynamic forces driving those laws are generated by the same
free-energy potential.

Classical flash calculations impose phase-equilibrium closures, commonly written
as fugacity equalities.  In the present notation the reversible transfer of
component $\alpha$ from phase $\xi$ to phase $\zeta$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\mu_\zeta^\alpha-\mu_\xi^\alpha=0,
	\label{eq:classical_phase_equilibrium_mu}
\end{equation}
%
which is the chemical-potential form of the fugacity-equality condition used in
isothermal phase equilibrium.  In classical compositional models the
$\mu_\xi^\alpha$ are therefore not solved from the conversion-potential evolution
law \eqref{eq:MC_admissible_conversion_component}.  Instead, they must be
defined constitutively from the chosen equation of state, composition variables,
and temperature.

The affinity is the stoichiometric projection of these phase/component
potentials:
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_{(m)}
	=-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha .
	\label{eq:classical_affinity_projection}
\end{equation}
%
For an equilibrated mechanism, imposing $\mc A_{(m)}=0$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi (m)}^\alpha=0,
	\label{eq:classical_equilibrium_affinity}
\end{equation}
%
which is the classical statement that the stoichiometric sum of chemical
potentials vanishes.  For a kinetically limited mechanism, such as a slow
fluid--solid reaction, \eqref{eq:classical_affinity_projection} instead supplies
the driving force in a closure
%
\begin{equation}
	\dot r_{(m)}=\mc K_{(m)}(\mc A_{(m)},\mbf\Lambda),
	\qquad \mc A_{(m)}\dot r_{(m)}\ge 0
	\label{eq:classical_kinetic_affinity_closure}
\end{equation}
%
with the sign convention used here.  Thus equilibrium chemistry, kinetic
reactive transport, and phase transformation all use the same stoichiometric
projection; only the closure for $\dot r_{(m)}$ changes.

The momentum equations contain an additional conversion term absent from purely
kinematic Darcy-scale compositional balances,
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\alpha\sum_\xi\dot c_\xi^\alpha
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
	\label{eq:conversion_momentum_interpretation}
\end{equation}
%
The term combines the inertial bookkeeping required when mass is removed from or
added to a moving phase with the gradient force generated by variation of the
current-rate constraint $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.  Total
mass conservation constrains the component source rates, but the explicit form
above avoids introducing an additional conversion-velocity notation.  These terms
vanish or are hidden in classical reservoir models when phase momenta are not
solved explicitly and the phase velocities are supplied by Darcy laws.

Finally, the remaining multipliers have standard constraint interpretations:
$\lambda$ enforces saturation of the pore volume by the phases,
$\pi_\xi$ enforces $\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1$, and $\tau_\xi^\alpha$ enforces
the phase/component storage relation.  The novelty of the present formulation is
not the stoichiometric source form itself, but the fact that it is embedded in a
phase-attached variational mechanics in which the Jacobian $J_\xi$ is varied.
That is what exposes the conversion-induced momentum production terms and gives
a direct bridge between mixture mechanics and classical compositional reactive
transport.

\subsection{Black-oil limit}
\label{sec:black_oil_limit}

The black-oil model is obtained by further specializing
\eqref{eq:classical_total_mass_component_balance}.  Take the fluid phases to be
water, oil, and gas, \(\mc F=\{w,o,g\}\), neglect mechanical dispersion and
molecular diffusion as above, use a fixed solid frame, and replace the full
component EOS by black-oil formation-volume factors and solution-gas data.  In
stock-tank units the phase/component storage factors are identified as
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar\rho_w\eta_w^W=\frac{\rho_{w,{\rm sc}}}{B_w},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_o\eta_o^O=\frac{\rho_{o,{\rm sc}}}{B_o},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_g\eta_g^G=\frac{\rho_{g,{\rm sc}}}{B_g},
	\qquad
	\bar\rho_o\eta_o^G=\frac{\rho_{g,{\rm sc}}R_s}{B_o}.
	\label{eq:black_oil_storage_identifications}
\end{equation}
%
Here \(W\), \(O\), and \(G\) label stock-tank water, oil, and gas components;
\(B_w\), \(B_o\), and \(B_g\) are formation-volume factors; \(R_s\) is the
dissolved gas-oil ratio, and the subscript \({\rm sc}\) denotes surface
conditions.  Let \(\mbf q_f=\phi S_f\mbf v_f\) be the phase volumetric flux and
let \(\mc Q_w\), \(\mc Q_o\), and \(\mc Q_g\) denote the corresponding
surface-volume source terms.  Division of the water, oil-component, and
gas-component balances by their corresponding surface densities gives
%
\begin{align}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi\frac{S_w}{B_w}\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\frac{\mbf q_w}{B_w}\right)
	 & = \mc Q_w,
	\label{eq:black_oil_water_balance} \\
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi\frac{S_o}{B_o}\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\frac{\mbf q_o}{B_o}\right)
	 & = \mc Q_o,
	\label{eq:black_oil_oil_balance}   \\
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left[
		\phi\left(\frac{S_g}{B_g}+R_s\frac{S_o}{B_o}\right)
		\right]
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left[
		\frac{\mbf q_g}{B_g}
		+R_s\frac{\mbf q_o}{B_o}
		\right]
	 & = \mc Q_g .
	\label{eq:black_oil_gas_balance}
\end{align}
%
These are the standard black-oil conservation laws: water is present only in the
water phase, the stock-tank oil component is present in the oil phase, and the
stock-tank gas component is present in the gas phase and dissolved in the oil
phase through \(R_s\).  The specialization removes the need for full
phase-composition unknowns; \(B_f\) and \(R_s\) are supplied as black-oil PVT
closures instead of being generated by a full compositional flash.

The phase fluxes follow from the same Darcy reduction used in
\eqref{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit}.  With
\(\bs\kappa_f=k_{rf}\bs\kappa\), where $k_{rf}$ is the relative permeability,
the fixed-frame flux closure is
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf q_f
	=
	-\frac{k_{rf}}{\mu_f}\bs\kappa
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g\right),
	\qquad
	f\in\{w,o,g\}.
	\label{eq:black_oil_darcy_flux}
\end{equation}
%
If oil pressure is chosen as the black-oil reference pressure, the general
capillary-pressure closure described above specializes to the usual water-oil and
gas-oil relations
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar p_w=\bar p_o-p_{cow}(S_w),
	\qquad
	\bar p_g=\bar p_o+p_{cgo}(S_g).
	\label{eq:black_oil_capillary_pressure_closures}
\end{equation}
%
These closures are then used with one pressure, for example \(\bar p_o\), and
two independent saturations because \(S_w+S_o+S_g=1\).  The remaining phase
pressures are reconstructed from \eqref{eq:black_oil_capillary_pressure_closures}
before evaluating \eqref{eq:black_oil_darcy_flux}.

\subsection{Single-solid, single-fluid Biot limit}
\label{sec:single_solid_single_fluid_biot_limit}

The classical Biot poromechanics equations are recovered by taking one solid
skeleton phase and one fluid pore phase, \(\mc S=\{s\}\) and \(\mc F=\{f\}\),
with no reactions or phase transformations.  There is then no fluid-fluid
interface, no capillary-pressure difference, and the equivalent pressure
coincides with the unique fluid pressure,
\(\bar p_E=\bar p_f\).  The solid pressure relation
\eqref{eq:MC_phase_equivalent_pressure} also gives
\(\bar p_s=\bar p_E\).  In this limit
\eqref{eq:MC_solid_biot_rollup_coefficient} reduces to \(B=B_s\), so the
nonlinear coefficient in \eqref{eq:MC_phase_biot_coefficient} is the
Biot coefficient of the single skeleton.

With conversion terms removed, the overall momentum balance
\eqref{eq:MC_overall_momentum_nonlinear_biot} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{\xi\in\{s,f\}}\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	=
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
	-B\bar p_f\mbf I
	\right)
	+\rho\mbf g .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_spatial_momentum}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the total Cauchy stress is the fixed-pressure skeleton stress minus the
Biot pressure correction.  For a quasi-static skeleton solve the inertial term
is dropped, giving the usual equilibrium equation
%
\begin{equation}
	\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(
	\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
	-B\bar p_f\mbf I
	\right)
	+\rho\mbf g=\mbf 0 .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_quasistatic_momentum}
\end{equation}
%
The fluid transport equation is the single-fluid specialization of the
solid-reference component balance.  Let
\(\mbf w_f=\phi_f\bar\rho_f\left(\mbf v_f-\mbf v_s\right)\) be the current
relative mass flux and let \(\mbf W_f\) be its Piola transform defined in
\eqref{eq:reference_relative_mass_flux}.  In
\eqref{eq:solid_reference_fluid_component_balance}, take the single component in
the single fluid phase to be the fluid itself.  Then \(\eta_f^f=1\), the
conversion source vanishes, and the component storage
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\eta_f^f\) reduces to the Lagrangian fluid mass
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).  The corresponding current-balance form is obtained from
\eqref{eq:component_spatial_mass} by the same specialization and by splitting
the fluid velocity into solid motion plus relative flux:
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\mbf v_s\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
Using the material derivative following the solid skeleton,
\(D_s/Dt=\partial/\partial t+\mbf v_s\cdot\nabla_{\mbf x}\), this
becomes
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{D_s}{Dt}\left(\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\phi_f\bar\rho_f\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
Since \(D_sJ/Dt=J\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s\), the first two terms
combine into the derivative of \(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\):
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{J}\frac{D_s}{Dt}
	\left(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf w_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
Multiplying by \(J\), using the divergence transform
\eqref{eq:solid_reference_flux_divergence}, and observing that
\(D_s/Dt=\partial/\partial t\) at fixed \(\mbf X\) gives
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass}:
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0 .
	\label{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the finite-deformation storage variable is the Lagrangian fluid mass
\(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).  Equivalently, with \(\Phi_f=J\phi_f\),
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\Phi_f\bar\rho_f\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
If the fluid is barotropic, \(\bar\rho_f=\bar\rho_f\left(\bar p_f\right)\), and
\({\rm d}\bar\rho_f/{\rm d}\bar p_f=\bar\rho_f/K_f\), where \(K_f\) is the
fluid bulk modulus, then
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass} can be written as
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar\rho_f
	\left[
		\frac{\partial \Phi_f}{\partial t}
		+\frac{\Phi_f}{K_f}
		\frac{\partial \bar p_f}{\partial t}
		\right]
	+\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
To reach the classical Biot storage equation, the finite-deformation porosity
law is also specialized to its standard poromechanics tangent form
\cite{coussy2004poromechanics}.  With \(B\) fixed over the increment, \(K_s\)
denoting the solid-grain bulk modulus, and \(M\) denoting the Biot storage
modulus,
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial\Phi_f}{\partial t}
	=
	B\frac{1}{J}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}
	+\frac{B-\Phi_f}{K_s}
	\frac{\partial\bar p_f}{\partial t},
	\qquad
	\frac{1}{M}
	=
	\frac{B-\Phi_f}{K_s}
	+\frac{\Phi_f}{K_f}.
\end{equation}
%
Substitution gives the finite-deformation pressure-storage balance
%
\begin{equation}
	B\frac{1}{J}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}
	+\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial\bar p_f}{\partial t}
	+\frac{1}{\bar\rho_f}\nabla_{\mbf X}\cdot\mbf W_f
	=0 .
\end{equation}
%
Finally, for small strain, \(J\simeq 1+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u\), and the
reference flux term reduces to the divergence of the current volumetric relative
flux \(\mbf q_f=\mbf w_f/\bar\rho_f\).  The Darcy specialization
\eqref{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit} gives, in the current configuration,
%
\begin{equation}
	\phi_f\left(\mbf v_f-\mbf v_s\right)
	=
	-\frac{\bs\kappa_f}{\mu_f}
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g\right).
	\label{eq:biot_limit_darcy_law}
\end{equation}
%
Equations \eqref{eq:biot_limit_quasistatic_momentum},
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_reference_fluid_mass}, and
\eqref{eq:biot_limit_darcy_law} are the finite-deformation single-fluid
poromechanics form of the model.

The familiar small-strain Biot equations follow by linearizing the skeleton
motion, using a linear elastic law for \(\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}\), and
substituting the Darcy flux directly into the fluid balance.  The source-free
reduced system is
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:linear_biot_limit}
	\begin{align}
		\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left(
		\bs\sigma^{\prime\prime}
		-B\bar p_f\mbf I
		\right)
		+\rho\mbf g
		 & =\mbf 0,
		\label{eq:linear_biot_momentum} \\
		\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial \bar p_f}{\partial t}
		+B\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
		\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf u\right)
		-\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
		\left[
			\frac{\bs\kappa_f}{\mu_f}
			\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_f-\bar\rho_f\mbf g\right)
			\right]
		 & =0.
		\label{eq:linear_biot_fluid_content}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
Here \(\mbf u\) is the small displacement of the skeleton.  The pressure
coupling in \eqref{eq:linear_biot_momentum} comes
from the same fixed-pressure Legendre stress split that produced
\eqref{eq:MC_phase_biot_split}; the fluid-content coupling
in \eqref{eq:linear_biot_fluid_content} is the small-strain form of the
finite-deformation storage \(J\phi_f\bar\rho_f\).  Thus the classical Biot system
is not an independent theory here, but the single-solid, single-fluid,
no-conversion, linearized limit of the variational mixture model.
