\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reservoir flow, reactive transport, and geomechanics are usually formulated
from different primary variables and with different levels of thermodynamic
resolution.  Compositional simulators evolve phase/component inventories and
close phase fluxes with Darcy laws, phase behavior, and capillary relations.
Poromechanics instead emphasizes deformation, pore pressure, and effective
stress, while electrochemical and geochemical models introduce charge,
diffusion, and reaction through additional constitutive laws.  These
specializations are effective in their intended regimes, but their direct
coupling can obscure whether a pressure, source, flux, or interaction force is
derived from a balance law, a constraint, or a dissipative closure.

Several established theories supply the ingredients needed for a more unified
description.  Reacting-mixture variational theories already provide
phase-attached motions, material conversion, constituent momentum production,
and Darcy-type porous-flow reductions
\cite{bedford1979variational,drumheller1980thermomechanical,bedford1983theories,drumheller2000}.
Macroscopic porous-medium thermodynamics derives multiphase balances,
capillary restrictions, and generalized transport laws from averaged energy
and entropy statements
\cite{hassanizadeh1993thermodynamic,gray1999thermodynamics}.  Internal-variable
extensions represent drainage--imbibition hysteresis as dissipative evolution
within the interfacial state \cite{wei2006capillary_hysteresis}.  Poromechanics
identifies effective-stress and equivalent-pressure structures
\cite{coussy2004poromechanics,kim2013rigorous}.  Seguin and Walkington give a
nonreactive multicomponent, multiphase poroelastic theory in a solid observer
\cite{seguin2019multicomponent}, and Foster and Xu derive a fixed-pressure
nonlinear Biot coefficient for a finite-deformation binary mixture
\cite{foster2025}.
For reacting and diffusing mixtures, force--flux restrictions follow from
continuum thermodynamics and Onsager stationarity
\cite{bothe2015continuum,doi2011onsager,wang2021onsager}; nonisothermal
chemical-potential gradients also require explicit attention to potential
gauges \cite{goldobin2016thermal}.  Finally, electroelastic potential theory
relates electric enthalpy, displacement, and Maxwell stress
\cite{mcmeeking2007virtual_work_electrostatic,dorfmann2017nonlinear_electroelasticity}.
Bennethum and Cushman derive the macroscale electroquasistatic field equations
for multicomponent, multiphase porous media \cite{bennethum2002electroI} and the corresponding constitutive restrictions for phase stresses, diffusion, and Darcy flow \cite{bennethum2002electroII}.

This paper integrates these structures in one open-system phase/component
derivation.  The resulting theory couples seven extensions of the established
balance, variational, thermodynamic, electrostatic, and reactive-solid
constructions.

The derivation has three steps with distinct roles.  A constrained
Hamilton--d'Alembert statement determines the phase-coordinate equations,
storage multipliers, momentum balances, and electrostatic field equation.  Its
virtual work carries prescribed loading and the d'Alembert work of constitutive
nonconservative forces.  A Coleman--Noll exploitation of two subsystem energy
balances and the entropy inequality determines the admissible stresses,
pressures, entropies, component potentials, and residual force--rate products.
Onsager stationarity then supplies near-equilibrium closures for saturation
redistribution, reaction, and component-relative transport.  In the dynamic
capillary branch, the saturation force is held fixed in the coordinate
variation and varied through its conjugate rate in the Onsager statement.  This
separation distinguishes balance and constraint equations from the
material-specific closures that determine the dissipative rates.

Within that construction, the paper makes seven specific contributions.
%
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The open-system momentum balance retains the momentum carried by mass
	      converted into each phase.  Its fluid reduction gives a generalized
	      Darcy law containing the actual phase-pressure gradients, body
	      force, Maxwell stress, inertia, and conversion-insertion momentum.  Net
	      phase conversion changes the algebraic resistance and mobility used to
	      eliminate the phase velocity; the exact nonsingularity and stronger
	      positive-resistance conditions are distinguished from dissipation of the
	      underlying drag.

	\item A fixed-equivalent-pressure Legendre transform extends the nonlinear
	      Biot construction from a binary mixture to multiple reacting solid
	      phases at finite deformation.  The phase Biot coefficient is evaluated
	      at the current reacted state and changes through solid mass,
	      composition,
	      distension, intrinsic specific volume, stress-free deformation, and
	      internal variables.

	\item The thermal and electrical construction gives gauge-invariant
	      electrochemical transport and reaction forces when the mobile-fluid and
	      solid subsystems have different temperatures.  The
	      temperature-weighted component-potential gradient remains separate from
	      the electric field, while the usual electrochemical-potential gradient
	      is recovered in the isothermal limit.

	\item The constrained interfacial energy gives the stored capillary pressure
	      at the current saturation and capillary-history state, with history
	      evolution selecting drainage, imbibition, and scanning paths; an
	      optional positive-semidefinite resistance adds a dynamic pressure lag
	      proportional to the skeleton-following saturation rate.  Because the
	      phases are derived as one coupled system, the phase electrical enthalpy
	      differences enter the capillary pressure relations as a correction that
	      uncoupled capillary closures omit.  The electric potential also
	      generates electric displacement, Maxwell stress, and dielectric
	      pressure, and these stored, electrical, and dissipative contributions
	      together set the actual phase pressures in the momentum and Darcy
	      equations and give the electrically corrected equivalent pore pressure.

	\item Variation of component mass fractions gives a generalized
	      composition-stationarity system.  At equilibrium it recovers equality of
	      the appropriate electrochemical potentials; away from equilibrium it
	      determines phase-reference values of the specific transfer-work
	      potentials.  Consequently, the
	      conversion force for phase change, dissolution, precipitation, and pore
	      filling differs from the traditional stoichiometric affinity by an
	      explicit phase-transfer-work term.

	\item A single phase/component balance structure governs the mobile phases
	      and the multiple load-bearing solid phases that share the skeleton
	      motion but retain phase-specific true deformation, distension,
	      stress-free maps, composition, and internal variables.  Pore-filling
	      conversion therefore appears as skeleton-attached solid mass that
	      changes phase volume and can alter stress-free deformation, elasticity,
	      and plastic flow, extending reactive-solid kinematics to an open
	      multiphase porous medium with simultaneous transport and pressure
	      coupling.

	\item The plastic dissipation fixes the stress that drives plastic flow:
	      plastic work is performed by the deviatoric intrinsic phase stress
	      mapped through the elastic true-deformation and distension factors,
	      and the nonlinear Biot transform relates this driving stress to the
	      fixed-pressure skeleton stress entering the momentum balance.  The
	      distension decomposition supplies a second, distinct driving stress,
	      so the isochoric and volumetric parts of the flow are each associated
	      with their own yield surface; the non-associativity that single-surface
	      effective-stress models must impose on the volumetric part is instead
	      carried by the separate distension yield surface.  Every mechanism is
	      therefore associated in its own stress space, which resolves the
	      classical non-associativity while preserving an associated flow rule
	      throughout, and deformation--distension laws remain admissible whenever
	      their combined conjugate power in the residual dissipation is
	      nonnegative.
\end{enumerate}
%
These features are intended to preserve a direct route to familiar engineering
models.  The theory is pulled back to the solid reference configuration and
then reduced explicitly to classical compositional flow, a black-oil model, a
Nernst--Planck--Darcy system, and finite- and small-deformation
single-solid/single-fluid Biot equations.  Each reduction states which
electrical, thermal, conversion, kinematic, or constitutive terms are removed.
The resulting correspondence checks the general formulation.  Application to a
particular material or reservoir additionally requires equations of state,
relative permeabilities, capillary-history evolution laws, kinetic data, and
numerical algorithms.

The scope is deliberately local and continuum-scale.  Constitutive functions
are assumed sufficiently smooth between phase and hysteresis switching events,
with positive phase densities, volume fractions, and temperatures.  Separate
switching conditions are needed when a phase or component appears or
disappears.  Continuous capillary-history variables encode saturation reversal
and phase trapping, while material-specific evolution laws select drainage,
imbibition, and scanning paths.  A dynamic pressure lag is admitted as an
optional dissipative closure for rapid saturation transients, including
fracture--matrix exchange in tight and shale reservoirs.  Independent
interfacial-area evolution remains outside the present state.  The
electroquasistatic state describes an isotropic
dielectric response without permanent polarization, dielectric relaxation, or
electromagnetic-wave dynamics.  Mass added to or removed from a phase carries
that phase's specific internal energy and entropy.  The general two-temperature
reaction force uses neutral transfer coefficients, which keep charged transfer
between the thermal subsystems independent of the electric-potential gauge.
The reaction rate and associated reaction-energy transfer may be related by a
joint linear near-equilibrium law with cross-coupling, provided their combined
entropy production is nonnegative for each mechanism.  These are
near-equilibrium laws.  Boundary~data, gauges, and compatibility conditions
are stated, but analysis of
well-posedness and numerical validation is deferred.

The phase/component mass and charge balances introduce their notation as it is
needed.  The Hamilton--d'Alembert construction is then developed, followed by
the constitutive restrictions and the Onsager closures they identify.
Section~\ref{sec:constitutive_models_entropy_inequality} introduces the solid
kinematics, performs the Coleman--Noll restriction, and derives the
dissipative closures, Biot transformations, and crystallization specialization.
The solid-reference equations are then collected for reservoir implementation,
and the final reductions establish correspondence with the classical models
listed above.  Appendix~\ref{sec:technical_setting} collects the detailed
observer, gauge, and admissible-data conditions.
