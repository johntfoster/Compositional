\section{Variational Derivation}
\label{sec:variational}

We derive the governing equations from Hamilton's extended principle, following Bedford \& Drumheller~\cite{drumheller1980thermomechanical}, adapted to a compositional mixture system containing phases (solid, aquous, oliec, and gas phases) and arbitrary miscible components $\alpha$ within each phase.

Each phase has volume fraction~$\phi_\xi$, intrinsic density~$\bar\rho_\xi$, bulk density $\rho_\xi = \phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi$, velocity~$\mbf{v}_\xi$, deformation gradient~$\mbf{F}_\xi$, and Jacobian $J_\xi = \det(\mbf{F}_\xi)$.

\subsection{Variational principle and its terms}

The extended Hamilton's principle is
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation*}
	\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left[\delta(T - U) + \delta W + \sum_k \delta C_k\right] dt = 0,
\end{equation*}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
with kinetic energy, internal energy, virtual work, and four constraints:
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	T        & = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}}\frac{1}{2} \rho_\xi \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \dv,
	\label{eq:T_def}                                                                                                                                                                                                \\
	U        & = \sum_{\xi}\int_{\mc{B}} \rho_{\xi}\,\psi_{\xi}(\mbf{F}_{\xi}, \bar\rho_{\xi}, \eta^\al_{\xi})\dv
	+ \int_{\mc{B}} \phi\,\gamma(S_a, S_g) \dv,
	\label{eq:U_def}                                                                                                                                                                                                \\
	\delta W & = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} (\rho_\xi\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_\xi)\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi \,\dv
	-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc B}L_\xi^\alpha
	\frac{\delta\mc C_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}\,\dv
	- \sum_m\int_{\mc B}\mc A_m\,\delta\dot r_m\,\dv,
	\label{eq:W_def}                                                                                                                                                                                                \\
	C_1      & = \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1\right) \,\dv,
	\label{eq:C1_def}                                                                                                                                                                                               \\
	C_2      & = \sum_\xi \sum_\al \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi 0}} \tau^\al_\xi \left( J_\xi - \frac{\phi_{\xi 0} \eta^\al_{\xi 0} \bar\rho_{\xi 0} + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \eta^\al_\xi \bar\rho_\xi} \right) \dV_\xi,
	\label{eq:C2_def}                                                                                                                                                                                               \\
	C_3      & = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \pi_\xi \left( \sum_\alpha \eta_\xi^\alpha - 1 \right) \dv,
	\label{eq:C3_def}                                                                                                                                                                                               \\
	C_4      & = \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc B}\mu_\xi^\alpha\left(\frac{\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}-\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m\right)\dv .
	\label{eq:C4_def}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The coefficients $L_\xi^\alpha$ are component conversion-work potentials
conjugate to adding component mass to phase $\xi$.  The affinities $\mc A_m$ are
introduced as generalized mechanism forces in the virtual-work one-form,
conjugate to the current progress-rate variations $\delta\dot r_m$.  The
kinematic constraint $C_4$ ties those progress rates to the phase-attached
accumulated sources through $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.
This distinction is essential:
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ is not a freely varied Eulerian scalar, because variation of
the phase map also varies $J_\xi$.  Chemical reactions and pure phase
transformations are both represented by the unified stoichiometric
parameterization \eqref{eq:unified_stoich_current}.  We treat
$\mbf{x}_\xi$, $\eta^\alpha_\xi$, $\phi_\xi$, $\bar\rho_\xi$, $\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha$,
$\dot r_m$, $\mu_\xi^\alpha$, and $\mc A_m$ as variational variables.  The
conversion-work potentials $L_\xi^\alpha$ are constitutive inputs, with
additional closure required for kinetically limited mechanisms.

\subsection{Extending Bedford and Drumheller identity 1.23}
\label{sec:extended_bd_identity}

The accumulated source $\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha$ is attached to the phase-$\xi$
reference coordinates, while its current production rate is
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.  Thus variations of source
terms must be pushed forward with the same phase map as the corresponding
component density.
%
For any phase field $\Gamma_\xi$, the Bedford--Drumheller identity extends componentwise as
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta \int_{\mc{B}} \rho^\alpha_\xi \Gamma_\xi \, \dv
	 & = \int_{\mc{B}} \left( \rho^\alpha_\xi\delta\Gamma_\xi
	+ \frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}\Gamma_\xi\right)\dv .
	\label{eq:bd128_mod2}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
summing both sides of \eqref{eq:bd128_mod2} over $\alpha$ gives
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta \int_{\mc{B}} \rho_\xi \psi_\xi \, \dv
	 & = \int_{\mc{B}} \left(\rho_\xi \delta \Gamma_\xi + \sum_\alpha \frac{\delta \mc{C}^\al_\xi}{J_\xi} \Gamma_\xi  \right)  \dv
	\label{eq:bd128_mod}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
where we've used $\rho_\xi = \sum_\alpha \rho^\alpha_\xi$.

\subsection{Variation of the kinetic energy}
\label{sec:var_T_app}
% ============================================================

The variation of kinetic energy using \eqref{eq:bd128_mod} is then
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\delta T = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \rho_\xi\,\mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \delta\mbf{v}_\xi + \frac{1}{2} \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \sum_\al \frac{\delta \mc{C}^\al_\xi}{J_\xi} \dv.
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Since $\delta\mbf{v}_\xi = \mathrm{d}(\delta\mbf{x}_\xi)/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi\vert_{t_1} = \delta\mbf{x}_\xi\vert_{t_2} = \mbf{0}$, integration by parts in time gives
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \delta T \dt
	= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \left(-\rho_\xi\,\mbf{a}_\xi - \sum_\al \dot c^\alpha_\xi \mbf{v}_\xi \right) \cdot \delta\mbf{x}_\xi + \frac{1}{2} \mbf{v}_\xi \cdot \mbf{v}_\xi \sum_\al \frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}  \dv \dt
	\label{eq:deltaT_app}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
\subsection{Variation of the phase free energies}
\label{sec:var_Uxi}
% ============================================================

For every phase, solid or mobile, let $\psi_\xi$ denote the specific Helmholtz
free energy.  The corresponding contribution to the total free energy is written
in the same current density-weighted form,
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	U_\xi & = \int_{\mc B}\rho_\xi\psi_\xi(\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^1, \eta_\xi^2, \dots, \eta_\xi^N)\,\dv \notag \\
	      & = \int_{\mc B}\rho_\xi\psi_\xi(\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta)\,\dv
	\label{eq:U_xi_uniform}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Here $\eta_\xi^\beta$ denotes the full list of component mass fractions
$\{\eta_\xi^1,\ldots,\eta_\xi^N\}$; the index $\beta$ is not summed in this
argument list.
The constitutive dependence on $\mbf F_\xi$ may be trivial for mobile phases,
but keeping it in the notation allows one calculation for all phases.  The local
free-energy variation is
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta\psi_\xi
	 & = \left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\mbf F_\xi}\right\vert_{\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}:\delta\mbf F_\xi
	+ \left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\bar\rho_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\delta\bar\rho_\xi
	+ \sum_\alpha
	\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^{\beta\neq\alpha}}
	\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha .
	\label{eq:delta_psi_uniform}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Using \eqref{eq:bd128_mod}, integrating the $\delta\mbf F_\xi$ term by parts,
and collecting independent variations gives
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta U_\xi
	 & = \sum_\xi\int_{\mc B}\Bigg[
		-\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma'_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf x_\xi
	+ \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\bar\rho_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\delta\bar\rho_\xi \notag \\
	 & \qquad
		+ \sum_\alpha \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^{\beta\neq\alpha}}\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha
		+ \sum_\alpha \psi_\xi\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
		\Bigg]\dv + \text{boundary terms}.
	\label{eq:deltaU_uniform}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The corresponding Cauchy stress is
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\bs\sigma'_\xi
	\equiv \rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\mbf F_\xi}\right\vert_{\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}\mbf F_\xi^T .
	\label{eq:var_cauchy_def}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
For mobile phases this term vanishes when $\psi_\xi$ has no dependence on
$\mbf F_\xi$.  For a solid pulled back to its reference configuration for
computation, one may introduce $W_{\xi_s}=J_{\xi_s}\rho_{\xi_s}\psi_{\xi_s}$ and
$\mbf P_{\xi_s}=J_{\xi_s}\bs\sigma'_{\xi_s}\mbf F_{\xi_s}^{-T}
	=\partial W_{\xi_s}/\partial\mbf F_{\xi_s}$ for the corresponding classical
reference energy density.

\subsection{Variation of the interfacial energies}
\label{sec:var_gamma}
% ============================================================

With three mobile phases (aqueous, oleic, and gas), let
\[
	\phi = \phi_a + \phi_o + \phi_g,
	\qquad
	S_a = \frac{\phi_a}{\phi},
	\qquad
	S_g = \frac{\phi_g}{\phi},
\]
so that $S_o = 1-S_a-S_g = \phi_o/\phi$.  The saturations are used here only as
ratios of the primitive volume-fraction fields; the variations are still taken
with respect to $\phi_a$, $\phi_o$, and $\phi_g$.  We specialize the interfacial energy density to
\[
	U_\gamma = \int_{\mc{B}} \phi\,\gamma\!\left(S_a,S_g\right)\,\dv.
\]
Following the saturation-based capillary-pressure construction used for the
two-mobile-phase case, but now taking oil as the reference mobile phase, define
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	p_{c,oa} \equiv -\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial S_a},
	\qquad
	p_{c,og} \equiv -\frac{\partial \gamma}{\partial S_g} .
	\label{eq:pc_def}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Here $p_{c,oa}=\bar p_o-\bar p_a$ is the oil--aqueous capillary pressure and
$p_{c,og}=\bar p_o-\bar p_g$ is the additional oil--gas capillary pressure.
Using $\delta\phi=\delta\phi_a+\delta\phi_o+\delta\phi_g$ and
\[
	\delta S_a=\frac{\delta\phi_a-S_a\delta\phi}{\phi},
	\qquad
	\delta S_g=\frac{\delta\phi_g-S_g\delta\phi}{\phi},
\]
the algebraic part of the interfacial variation can be written as
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta\!\left(\phi\gamma\right)
	 & = \gamma\,\delta\phi
	+ \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial S_a}\left(\delta\phi_a-S_a\delta\phi\right)
	+ \frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial S_g}\left(\delta\phi_g-S_g\delta\phi\right) \notag \\
	 & = \gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g,
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
where
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\gamma_o & = \gamma + S_a p_{c,oa}+S_g p_{c,og}, \notag \\
	\gamma_a & = \gamma_o-p_{c,oa}, \notag                  \\
	\gamma_g & = \gamma_o-p_{c,og}.
	\label{eq:primitive_gamma_derivatives}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Consequently the capillary pressures are the oil-referenced differences of the
primitive interfacial potentials,
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	p_{c,oa} \equiv \bar p_o-\bar p_a \equiv \gamma_o-\gamma_a,
	\qquad
	p_{c,og} \equiv \bar p_o-\bar p_g \equiv \gamma_o-\gamma_g .
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%

The spatial fields must be varied using the inter-species transport identity.
For the phase displacement $\delta\mbf x_\xi$, the volume-fraction variation
contains the transported part
\[
	\delta_x\phi_\xi = -\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot(\phi_\xi\delta\mbf x_\xi),
\]
in addition to the independent coefficient variation used in the algebraic
Euler--Lagrange equations.  Consequently,
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta U_\gamma
	 & = \int_{\mc B}\left(\gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g\right)\dv \notag \\
	 & \quad
	+ \int_{\mc B}\left[\phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a\cdot\delta\mbf x_a
		+ \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_o\cdot\delta\mbf x_o
		+ \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_g\cdot\delta\mbf x_g\right]\dv
	+ \text{boundary terms}.
	\label{eq:deltaU_fg}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
This form keeps the gradient terms generated by the spatial dependence of the
volume fractions.  They cancel against the corresponding multiplier gradients in
the phase momentum balances after the volume-fraction Euler--Lagrange equations
are used.

\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_1$: volume-fraction}
\label{sec:var_C1_app}
% ============================================================

The constraint $C_1 = \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1)\,\dv$ is written in the current configuration, so the natural variation is Eulerian, i.e. taken at fixed spatial point $\mbf{x}$.  Since each $\phi_\xi$ is a constituent-attached spatial field, we use the Bedford--Drumheller identity
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\left.\delta\phi_\xi\right\vert_{\mbf{x}}
	= \delta\phi_\xi - \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi,
	\label{eq:delta_phi_BD_local}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
to express the variation in terms of the spatial variation $\delta\phi_\xi$ and the motion variation $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$. Therefore
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta C_1
	 & = \int_{\mc{B}} \delta\lambda \left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi - 1\right) \dv
	+ \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \lambda\left(\delta\phi_\xi - \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_\xi\right) \dv.
	\label{eq:deltaC1_app}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The first term enforces the saturation constraint $\sum_\xi\phi_\xi = 1$. The remaining terms contribute $\lambda$ to each $\delta\phi_\xi$ equation and $-\lambda\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\phi_\xi$ to each momentum variation $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$.

\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_2$: component material mass balance}
\label{sec:var_C2_app}
% ============================================================

The component material mass balance constraint is
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	C_2 = \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}\tau_\xi^\alpha \left(J_\xi - \frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0} \eta^\al_{\xi 0} + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha} \right) \,dV_\xi.
	\label{eq:C2_component_measure}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Hence
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta C_2
	 & = \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\tau_\xi^\alpha \delta J_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
	\notag                                        \\
	 & \quad
	+ \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi^2\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha}\,\delta\phi_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
	\notag                                        \\
	 & \quad
	+ \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi^2 \eta^\al_\xi}\,\delta\bar\rho_\xi \, {\rm d}V_\xi
	\notag                                        \\
	 & \quad
	+ \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\tau_\xi^\alpha\,\frac{\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha + \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi \bar\rho_\xi (\eta^\al_\xi)^2}\,\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha \, {\rm d}V_\xi
	\notag                                        \\
	 & \quad
	- \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi \eta_\xi^\alpha}\,\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha \, {\rm d}V_\xi.
	\label{eq:deltaC2_lines}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Using $\dv = J_\xi {\rm d}V_\xi$ and the constraint $\phi_{\xi0}\bar\rho_{\xi0}\eta_{\xi0}^\alpha+\mc{C}_\xi^\alpha=J_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha$, the individual contributions push forward as follows.

\paragraph{$\delta J_\xi$ terms (from $\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$):}
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}_{\xi0}}
	\tau_\xi^\alpha \delta J_\xi \, dV_\xi
	 & = -\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}}
	\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\tau_\xi^\alpha \cdot \delta\mbf{x}_\xi \, \dv
	+ \text{b.t.}
	\label{eq:C2_dJ}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%

\paragraph{Spatial field terms:}
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta C_2 \supset \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc{B}}
	\left[
		\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi}\delta\phi_\xi
		+ \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\bar\rho_\xi}\delta\bar\rho_\xi
		+ \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha
		- \frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha}\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
		\right]\dv .
	\label{eq:C2_spatial_push}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%

\subsection{Variation of constraint $C_3$: mass partition within phase}
\label{sec:var_C3_app}
% ============================================================

%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\delta C_3
	 & = \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \delta\pi_\xi \left(\sum_\al \eta_\xi^\al - 1\right) \dv

The first line vanishes on the constraint surface $\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha = 1$ and recovers it as an Euler--Lagrange equation.  The second line contributes $\pi_\xi$ to each $\delta\eta_\xi^\alpha$ Euler--Lagrange equation.

\subsection{Stoichiometric conversion variations}
\label{sec:stoich_conversion_variations}
% ============================================================
Conversion sources are restricted by the current-rate kinematic constraint
\eqref{eq:C4_def}.  Define the component conversion potential coefficient
%
The variation of $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$ must be taken before
using the stoichiometric projection, because the denominator depends on the phase
map.  Repeating the transport calculation used for phase-attached rates gives
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\delta C_4\,\dt
	 & =\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\delta\mu_\xi^\alpha\left(\dot c_\xi^\alpha-
	\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m\right)\dv\dt \notag     \\
	 & \quad+\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\left[-\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}
		+\dot c_\xi^\alpha\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha\cdot\delta\mbf x_\xi\right]
	\dv\dt \notag                                            \\
	 & \quad-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{\mc B}\sum_m
	\left(\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)
	\delta\dot r_m\,\dv\dt+\text{b.t.}
	\label{eq:C4_variation}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The affinity is not introduced by allowing $\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ to vary freely.
The component-level conversion work has already entered through
\eqref{eq:W_def}; $\mc A_m$ is the generalized mechanism force conjugate to
the progress-rate variation $\delta\dot r_m$.  Its stoichiometric
identification is obtained below as the Euler--Lagrange equation associated with
$\dot r_m$.

Thus $\mu_\xi^\alpha$ is a kinematic multiplier enforcing the phase-attached
push-forward and stoichiometry, while $\mc A_m$ is the thermodynamic force
conjugate to mechanism progress.  The term
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha\nabla\mu_\xi^\alpha$ is the part of the mechanical coupling
that would be lost if $\delta\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ were treated as a free Eulerian
variation.

\subsection{Collected variational principle}
\label{sec:collected_var}
% ============================================================

Evaluating all variations and collecting by independent variations
$\delta\mbf{x}_\xi$, $\delta\phi_\xi$, $\delta\bar\rho_\xi$,
$\delta\eta^\alpha_\xi$, and $\delta\dot r_m$ gives the following reduced
collection.  In the displacement bracket, the raw multiplier combination
$-\lambda\nabla_{\mbf x}\phi_\xi-\nabla_{\mbf x}\sum_\alpha\tau_\xi^\alpha$
has been combined into $\phi_\xi\nabla_{\mbf x}\lambda$ using the corresponding
volume-fraction coefficient; the mobile-phase capillary terms are displayed
separately.
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	0 = & \sum_{\xi} \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
	-\rho_{\xi}\mbf{a}_{\xi}
	+ \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\cdot\bs\sigma'_{\xi} + \rho_{\xi}\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_{\xi}
		+ \phi_{\xi}\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\lambda
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_{\xi}^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf{v}_{\xi}\right)
	\Bigg]\cdot\delta\mbf{x}_{\xi} \,\dv \notag                                                                                                    \\
	    & - \int_{\mc B}\left[
		\phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a\cdot\delta\mbf x_a
		+ \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_o\cdot\delta\mbf x_o
		+ \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_g\cdot\delta\mbf x_g
	\right]\dv \notag                                                                                                                              \\
	    & + \sum_\xi \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
		\lambda + \sum_\alpha \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\phi_{\xi}}
	\Bigg] \delta\phi_{\xi} \,\dv \notag                                                                                                           \\
	    & - \int_{\mc B}\left(\gamma_a\delta\phi_a+\gamma_o\delta\phi_o+\gamma_g\delta\phi_g\right)\dv \notag                                      \\
	    & + \sum_{\xi}\int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
		-\rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}\right\vert_{\mbf{F}_{\xi},\eta_\xi^\beta}
		+ \sum_\alpha\frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}
	\Bigg] \delta\bar\rho_{\xi} \,\dv \notag                                                                                                       \\
	    & + \sum_{\xi}\sum_\alpha \int_{\mc{B}} \Bigg[
		\frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha} + \pi_{\xi}
		- \rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta^\alpha_\xi}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta^{\beta\neq\alpha}_\xi}
	\Bigg] \delta\eta^\alpha_\xi \,\dv \notag                                                                                                      \\
	    & + \sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\int_{\mc B}\left[
		\tfrac12\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi-\psi_\xi-L_\xi^\alpha
		-\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
		-\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha}
	\right]\frac{\delta \mc{C}_\xi^\alpha}{J_\xi}\,\dv \notag                                                                                      \\
	    & - \sum_m\int_{\mc B}\left(\mc A_m+\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\right)\delta\dot r_m\,\dv + \text{boundary terms}.
	\label{eq:collected_full}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The multiplier variations are omitted here because they return the constraint equations~\eqref{eq:C1_def}--\eqref{eq:C4_def}. Since all variations are considered arbitrary, each bracketed integrand must vanish pointwise, yielding the Euler--Lagrange equations.

\subsection{Euler--Lagrange equations}

The Euler--Lagrange equations generated by arbitrary $\delta\phi_\xi$ give,
for phases without an explicit capillary contribution,
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\phi_\xi \lambda = - \sum_\alpha \tau^\al_\xi .
	\label{eq:el_lambda}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
For the mobile phases the corresponding equations contain the primitive
capillary potentials discussed below.

The displacement equation obtained from the uniform part of the collected
principle is
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\rho_{\xi}\mbf{a}_{\xi} =
	\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\cdot\bs\sigma'_{\xi} + \rho_{\xi}\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_{\xi}
	+ \phi_{\xi} \nabla_{\mbf{x}}\lambda
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_{\xi}^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf{v}_{\xi}\right),
	\label{eq:el_momentum_uniform}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
with the mobile-phase capillary-force additions displayed separately below.

The Euler--Lagrange equation associated with arbitrary $\delta\bar\rho_\xi$ gives
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\al \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\al}{\phi_{\xi}}
	= \bar\rho^2_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\bar\rho_{\xi}}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\eta_\xi^\beta}
	\equiv \bar{p}_{\xi} .
	\label{eq:phase_pressure_all}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
These two Euler--Lagrange relations also recover the capillary-pressure
construction for the mobile phases.  Let $l\in\{a,o,g\}$ denote a mobile phase.
The coefficient of $\delta\phi_l$ in \eqref{eq:collected_full} includes the
primitive interfacial potential $\gamma_l$, so the mobile-phase volume-fraction
relation is
%
\[
	\lambda+\sum_\alpha\frac{\tau_l^\alpha}{\phi_l}-\gamma_l=0.
\]
%
Using \eqref{eq:phase_pressure_all} gives
%
\[
	\lambda+\bar p_l-\gamma_l=0,
	\qquad\hbox{or}\qquad
	\bar p_l=\gamma_l-\lambda .
\]
%
Consequently pressure differences between mobile phases are exactly the
differences of the primitive interfacial potentials,
%
\[
	\bar p_l-\bar p_k=\gamma_l-\gamma_k .
\]
%
For the oil-referenced definitions above, this gives
%
\[
	\bar p_o-\bar p_a=\gamma_o-\gamma_a=p_{c,oa},
	\qquad
	\bar p_o-\bar p_g=\gamma_o-\gamma_g=p_{c,og}.
\]
%
The same relations identify the equivalent pore pressure associated with the
volume constraint.  Define
%
\[
	\bar p_E\equiv-\lambda .
\]
%
Multiplying $\bar p_l=\gamma_l+\bar p_E$ by the mobile saturations and summing
over mobile phases gives
%
\[
	\sum_l S_l\bar p_l
	=
	\bar p_E+\sum_l S_l\gamma_l .
\]
%
The primitive potentials satisfy
%
\[
	\sum_l S_l\gamma_l
	=
	S_a(\gamma_o-p_{c,oa})+S_o\gamma_o+S_g(\gamma_o-p_{c,og})
	=
	\gamma .
\]
%
Therefore
%
\[
	\bar p_E=\sum_l S_l\bar p_l-\gamma .
\]
%
The Euler--Lagrange equations associated with arbitrary $\delta\eta_{\xi}^\alpha$ give
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	0 = \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha} + \pi_{\xi}
	- \rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\beta\neq\alpha}},
	\qquad \alpha=1,\ldots,N,
	\label{eq:el_eta_all}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
while variation with respect to $\pi_{\xi}$ returns the composition normalization
constraint
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{\alpha=1}^N \eta_{\xi}^\alpha = 1.
	\label{eq:el_eta_constraint}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Thus $\pi_{\xi}$ is the phase-level multiplier that enforces the redundant
composition coordinate.  Equation~\eqref{eq:el_eta_all} may be read
componentwise as
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{equation}
	\rho_{\xi}\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\beta\neq\alpha}}
	- \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}
	= \pi_{\xi},
	\label{eq:pi_componentwise}
\end{equation}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\rho_{\xi}\left(
	\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq\alpha}}
	- \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^\beta}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq\beta}}
	\right)
	 & = \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\alpha}{\eta_{\xi}^\alpha}
	- \frac{\tau_{\xi}^\beta}{\eta_{\xi}^\beta}.
	\label{eq:pi_difference}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Here $\kappa$ denotes the collection of composition components held fixed in the
partial derivative; it is not summed.

Choosing $\alpha=1$ and comparing against $j=2,3,\ldots,N$ gives a system of
$N-1$ equations,
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\rho_{\xi}\left(
	\left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^1}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq 1}}
	- \left.\p{\psi_{\xi}}{\eta_{\xi}^j}\right\vert_{\mbf F_\xi,\bar\rho_{\xi},\eta_{\xi}^{\kappa\neq j}}
	\right)
	 & = \frac{\tau_{\xi}^1}{\eta_{\xi}^1}
	- \frac{\tau_{\xi}^j}{\eta_{\xi}^j},
	\qquad j=2,\ldots,N.
	\label{eq:pi_difference_system}
\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
The equations associated with the conversion variables are
%
% AGENT-LOCK-BEGIN equation-lock: fixed through rendered equation (49); do not edit unless explicitly unlocked by user
\begin{align}
	\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
	                                                              & = \frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
	-\psi_\xi
	-L_\xi^\alpha
	-\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha},
	\label{eq:el_conversion_component}                                                                     \\
		\mc A_m                                                       & =-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha,
		\qquad m=1,\ldots,N_m.
		\label{eq:el_conversion_affinity}
	\end{align}
% AGENT-LOCK-END
%
Equation~\eqref{eq:el_conversion_affinity} is not an additional closure relation
for the state variables.  It defines the mechanism affinity as the stoichiometric
projection of the phase-attached conversion potentials, but only after the
kinematic push-forward $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$ has been
varied.  The closure enters only when one prescribes either an equilibrium
condition $\mc A_m=0$ or a kinetic relation
$\dot r_m=\mc K_m(\mc A_m,\text{state})$.  Thus, for kinetically limited
mechanisms such as slow fluid--solid reactions, \eqref{eq:el_conversion_affinity}
supplies the driving force rather than an algebraic equilibrium equation.  For an
instantaneous phase transformation, $\dot r_m$ may be a
measure in time.  In all cases, the phase momentum equations retain the inertial
production term and the $\nabla\mu_\xi^\alpha$ force generated by adding or
removing mass in a phase moving with velocity $\mbf v_\xi$.  For a
single-component solid, $\eta_{\xi_s}^\alpha=1$.

At this variational stage $L_\xi^\alpha$ remains an explicit constitutive
conversion-work potential.  An admissible choice of $L_\xi^\alpha$ is identified
by the Coleman--Noll argument in
Section~\ref{sec:constitutive_models_entropy_inequality}.

\subsection{Mobile-phase momentum equations}
\label{sec:mobile_phase_momentum}

For the aqueous phase, the capillary contribution gives the intermediate form
%
\begin{align}
	\rho_a\mbf a_a
	 & = \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma'_a + \rho_a\mbf g + \mbf b_a
	+ \phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\lambda
	-\phi_a\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_a
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_a^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_a^\alpha-\mbf{v}_a\right).
	\label{eq:el_mom_a_start}
\end{align}
%
The oleic and gas equations have the same form with $a$ replaced by $o$ or $g$.
The important point is that the interfacial variation contributes gradient terms
$\phi_\xi\nabla\gamma_\xi$, not merely algebraic multiples of
$\nabla\phi_\xi$.  These terms must be retained until the volume-fraction
pressure relations are substituted.  For a mobile phase,
$\lambda+\bar p_\xi-\gamma_\xi=0$, so
$\phi_\xi(\nabla\lambda-\nabla\gamma_\xi)=-\phi_\xi\nabla\bar p_\xi$.  The
standard phase pressure-gradient form is therefore
%
\begin{align}
	\rho_a\mbf{a}_a
	 & = \rho_a\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_a
	- \phi_a\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_a
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_a^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_a^\alpha-\mbf{v}_a\right),
	\label{eq:el_mom_a_simplified} \\
	\rho_o\mbf{a}_o
	 & = \rho_o\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_o
	- \phi_o\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_o
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_o^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_o^\alpha-\mbf{v}_o\right),
	\label{eq:el_mom_o_simplified} \\
	\rho_g\mbf{a}_g
	 & = \rho_g\mbf{g} + \mbf{b}_g
	- \phi_g\nabla_{\mbf{x}}\bar p_g
	+ \sum_\alpha \dot c_g^{\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_g^\alpha-\mbf{v}_g\right).
	\label{eq:el_mom_g_simplified}
\end{align}

\section{Constitutive Models and the Entropy Inequality}
\label{sec:multicomponent_solid_mixture_rules}
\label{sec:constitutive_models_entropy_inequality}

The present notation allows the solid skeleton to be treated as a single solid
phase, denoted here by $s$, while still resolving the mineral or solid components
through the mass fractions $\eta_s^\alpha$.  This is useful when reactions
convert one solid mineral into another but the products remain part of the same
load-bearing skeleton and share the same macroscopic deformation map
$\mbf x_s=\bs\chi_s(\mbf X_s,t)$.  In that case the solid free energy can be
chosen so that the component composition only enters through composition-weighted
moduli.  This recovers the mixture rules used in the earlier reacting-mixture
formulation, but avoids introducing a separate mechanical phase for each solid
mineral.

For the present fully compressible solid model we still need the distension
kinematics before it is meaningful to define an intrinsic stress.  The solid
phase has a macroscopic skeleton deformation $\mbf F_s$, but the stress carried
by a solid component is conjugate to the true, or undistended, deformation.  We
therefore introduce a solid distension tensor $\mbf A_s$ and write
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf F_s=\mbf A_s\bar{\mbf F}_s,
	\qquad
	J_s=a_s\bar J_s,
	\qquad
	a_s=\det\mbf A_s,
	\qquad
	\bar J_s=\det\bar{\mbf F}_s .
	\label{eq:solid_distension_decomposition}
\end{equation}
%
Here $\bar{\mbf F}_s$ is the true deformation seen by the load-bearing solid
material, while $\mbf A_s$ accounts for the change in the relation between the
solid material volume and the mixture volume.  In the isotropic specialization
one may set $\mbf A_s=a_s^{1/3}\mbf I$, giving
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\mbf F}_s=a_s^{-1/3}\mbf F_s .
	\label{eq:isotropic_distension_fbar}
\end{equation}
%
This is not the introduction of a separate distension energy.  It is the
kinematic device needed to separate deformation of the solid material from
changes in pore-space allocation, packing, and reaction-induced filling.  The
evolution of $a_s$ follows from the same mass-balance argument used by
Drumheller and in the earlier reacting-mixture formulation.  Summing the
component material balances over solid components gives the phase mass balance
%
\begin{equation}
	J_s\phi_s\bar\rho_s
	=\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}+c_s,
	\qquad
	c_s=\sum_\alpha c_s^\alpha .
	\label{eq:solid_material_mass_distension}
\end{equation}
%
Using $J_s=a_s\bar J_s$ and imposing true local conservation of mass for the
solid material,
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar J_s\bar\rho_s=\bar\rho_{s0},
	\label{eq:solid_true_mass_conservation}
\end{equation}
%
transforms \eqref{eq:solid_material_mass_distension} into
%
\begin{equation}
	a_s\phi_s\bar\rho_{s0}
	=\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}+c_s .
	\label{eq:solid_distension_mass_relation}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the solid volume fraction is tied directly to the motion only when there is
no mass exchange.  Taking the material time derivative of $J_s=a_s\bar J_s$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{D_s a_s/Dt}{a_s}
	=\frac{D_s J_s/Dt}{J_s}-\frac{D_s\bar J_s/Dt}{\bar J_s} .
\end{equation}
%
Since $D_sJ_s/Dt=J_s\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s$ and
\eqref{eq:solid_true_mass_conservation} implies
$(D_s\bar J_s/Dt)/\bar J_s=-(D_s\bar\rho_s/Dt)/\bar\rho_s$, the distension
evolution law is
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{a_s}\frac{D_s a_s}{Dt}
	=\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s
	+\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s}\frac{D_s\bar\rho_s}{Dt} .
	\label{eq:solid_distension_evolution}
\end{equation}
%
For incompressible solid constituents, $D_s\bar\rho_s/Dt=0$, and therefore
$D_s a_s/Dt=a_s\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\mbf v_s$.  If additionally $a_s=J_s$ in the
reference state, then $a_s=J_s$ for all time; for compressible multicomponent
solids, however, the intrinsic-density term makes $a_s$ evolve independently of
$J_s$.

For non-isothermal problems we follow the thermal split used in finite-strain
thermo-hydro-mechanics, but apply it to the true deformation rather than to the
total skeleton deformation.  Thus
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\mbf F}_s=\bar{\mbf F}_s^M\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta,
	\qquad
	\bar{\mbf F}_s^M=\bar{\mbf F}_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1},
	\label{eq:true_thermal_split}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta$ maps to the thermal stress-free true-solid
configuration and $\bar{\mbf F}_s^M$ is the mechanical part that enters the
intrinsic stresses.  For isotropic thermal expansion,
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta
	=\exp\left[\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\alpha_{\rm sk}(\vartheta,\phi_s^\beta)\,d\vartheta\right]\mbf I,
	\qquad
	\bar J_s^\theta
	=\exp\left[3\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\alpha_{\rm sk}(\vartheta,\phi_s^\beta)\,d\vartheta\right].
	\label{eq:thermal_expansion_split}
\end{equation}
%
Here $\alpha_{\rm sk}$ is a skeleton thermal expansion coefficient that may depend
on the solid composition.  This is the single-skeleton analogue of assigning
different thermal expansion behavior to different minerals: the composition
updates $\alpha_{\rm sk}$, while all solid components remain attached to the same
true deformation field.

This thermal split does not break the energy-based structure.  It is a
constitutive reparametrization of the free energy: instead of depending on
$\bar{\mbf F}_s$ directly, the elastic part of the free energy depends on
$\bar{\mbf F}_s^M=\bar{\mbf F}_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta)^{-1}$ and on $\theta$.  The
phase label remains part of the component Helmholtz energy: component $\alpha$ in
the solid phase need not have the same free energy as component $\alpha$ in a
liquid or gas phase.  The multicomponent solid free energy is written as
%
\begin{equation}
	\psi_s(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta)
	=\sum_\alpha \eta_s^\alpha\,
	\psi_s^\alpha(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,\bar\rho_s,a_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta),
	\label{eq:solid_multicomponent_free_energy}
\end{equation}
%
where $\eta_s^\beta$ denotes the full list of solid component mass fractions.
Temperature dependence of the free energy supplies entropy and heat capacity in
the usual way, while the dependence of $\bar{\mbf F}_s^\theta$ on $\theta$ and
the component volume fractions $\phi_s^\beta$ encodes stress-free thermal
expansion.  For example, water in a hydrate or ice-like solid would be
represented by $\psi_s^{\mathrm{H_2O}}$, whereas liquid water would have a
different function $\psi_\ell^{\mathrm{H_2O}}$.  The thermodynamic restrictions
that make these constitutive choices admissible are obtained next from the
entropy inequality.

\subsection{Solid mixture rules and nonlinear Biot coefficient}
\label{sec:solid_mixture_rules_nonlinear_biot}

The Coleman--Noll restrictions above close the solid constitutive structure in
terms of the component Helmholtz energies.  For the single load-bearing skeleton,
the component volume fractions, mass fractions, and intrinsic densities satisfy
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_final_density_mixture_rules}
	\begin{align}
		\bar\rho_s
		 & =\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s^\alpha, \\
		\eta_s^\alpha
		 & =\frac{\phi_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s^\alpha}{\bar\rho_s}, \\
		\phi_s^\alpha
		 & =\frac{\eta_s^\alpha\bar\rho_s}{\bar\rho_s^\alpha}.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
The solid Helmholtz energy is the mass-fraction average
\eqref{eq:solid_multicomponent_free_energy}, while the intrinsic skeleton stress
is the component volume-fraction average
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_final_stress_mixture_rule}
	\begin{align}
		\bar{\bs\sigma}_s'
		 & =\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^{\prime\alpha}, \\
		\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^{\prime\alpha}
		 & =\bar\rho_s^\alpha
		\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{\bar{\mbf F}_s^M}\heldfixed{\mc S}
		(\bar{\mbf F}_s^M)^T, \\
		\bs\sigma_s'
		 & =\phi_s\bar{\bs\sigma}_s' .
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
For the pressure and Biot split below, the Coleman--Noll calculation resolves
solid density dependence through the physical intrinsic density
$\bar\rho_s^\alpha$ of each solid component.  With this specialization, the
intrinsic solid pressure is the component-volume-fraction average of the
component pressures, and the distension force is
%
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{align}
		\bar p_s
		 & =\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha\bar p_s^\alpha,
		\label{eq:solid_final_intrinsic_pressure} \\
		\bar p_s^\alpha
		 & =\left(\bar\rho_s^\alpha\right)^2
		\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{\bar\rho_s^\alpha}\right\vert_{\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,J_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta},
		\label{eq:solid_final_component_pressure} \\
		\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')
		 & =\rho_s a_s\sum_\alpha\eta_s^\alpha
		\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{a_s}\heldfixed{\mc S}.
		\label{eq:solid_final_distension_pressure}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
These are not independent closure equations for double-prime stress.  They are
mixture rules generated by the same Helmholtz energies that supply entropy,
temperature dependence, composition dependence, and the reversible distension
response.

The same structure gives the overall momentum equation.  Summing the phase
momentum balances, using the mobile-phase pressure relations, and retaining the
conversion-momentum terms gives
%
\begin{align}
	\sum_\xi\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	= & \; \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma_s'
	+\rho\mbf g+\sum_\xi\mbf b_\xi
	-\phi_s\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_E
	-\sum_l\phi_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l
	\notag                                                        \\
	 & +\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\dot c_\xi^\alpha\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right),
	\label{eq:overall_momentum_phase_pressure_form}
\end{align}
%
where $l$ ranges over the mobile pore phases and
$\rho=\sum_\xi\rho_\xi$.  This is the pressure form of the capillary force.  The
primitive potentials $\gamma_l$ are useful in the
variation, but the volume-fraction Euler--Lagrange relation gives
$\lambda+\bar p_l-\gamma_l=0$ and therefore
$\phi_l(\nabla_{\mbf x}\lambda-\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_l)
=-\phi_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l$.  The final balance is therefore written in
terms of phase pressure gradients, which is the form used in compositional flow.
For a closed mixture with pairwise internal interactions,
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\xi\mbf b_\xi=\mbf 0 .
	\label{eq:internal_momentum_supply_sum}
\end{equation}
%
The same Euler--Lagrange relations also give the pressure-gradient collapse
needed to compare with the equivalent-pressure form used in multiphase
poromechanics.  Since $\bar p_l=\gamma_l+\bar p_E$, $\phi_l=\phi S_l$, and
$\gamma=\sum_l S_l\gamma_l$, the Gibbs--Duhem identity for the homogeneous
interfacial energy gives $\sum_l S_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\gamma_l=\mbf 0$.  Hence
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_l S_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l
	=\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_E .
	\label{eq:mobile_pressure_gradient_equivalent_pressure}
\end{equation}
%
Using this identity in \eqref{eq:overall_momentum_phase_pressure_form} gives
%
\begin{align*}
	\sum_\xi\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	= & \; \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\bs\sigma_s'
	+\rho\mbf g
	-\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_E
	\\
	 & +\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\dot c_\xi^\alpha\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
\end{align*}
%
If external momentum is supplied to the mixture, the omitted term
$\sum_\xi\mbf b_\xi$ is added to the right-hand side.

To connect this result with finite-deformation poromechanics, do the pressure
transform at the solid-component level.  Unlike a miscible fluid phase, a solid
skeleton component is a physical constituent with its own intrinsic density and
intrinsic specific volume,
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_intrinsic_specific_volume}
	\begin{align}
		\bar v_s^\alpha
		 & =\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s^\alpha}, \\
		\bar v_{s0}^\alpha
		 & =\frac{1}{\bar\rho_{s0}^\alpha}.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
The Coleman--Noll density coefficient has already identified the thermodynamic
component pressure in \eqref{eq:solid_final_component_pressure}.  Equivalently,
since $\bar v_s^\alpha=1/\bar\rho_s^\alpha$,
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar p_s^\alpha
	=-\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{\bar v_s^\alpha}\right\vert_{\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,J_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta}.
	\label{eq:solid_component_pressure_density}
\end{equation}
%
The storage multipliers may be normalized by the current volume occupied by each
solid component,
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar p_s^\alpha
	=\frac{\tau_s^\alpha}{\phi_s\phi_s^\alpha}.
	\label{eq:solid_component_storage_pressure_definition}
\end{equation}
%
This is a normalization of the multiplier, not a separate variation with respect
to $\bar\rho_s^\alpha$; the mixture pressure is still the average in
\eqref{eq:solid_final_intrinsic_pressure}.  The component-density dependence has
already been accounted for in the Coleman--Noll chain rule.  The component
Legendre transform is therefore
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_legendre_transform_pressure}
	\begin{align}
		\widetilde\psi_s^\alpha
		 & =\psi_s^\alpha+\bar p_s^\alpha\bar v_s^\alpha, \\
		\left.\p{\widetilde\psi_s^\alpha}{\bar p_s^\alpha}\right\vert_{\bar{\mbf F}_s^M,J_s,\theta,\eta_s^\beta}
		 & =\bar v_s^\alpha .
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
The pressure in the saturation constraint appears only after the component
pressures are summed.  Since the solid phase carries no mobile capillary
potential, \eqref{eq:el_lambda} gives
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_pressure_equivalent_pressure}
	\begin{align}
		\phi_s\lambda
		 & =-\sum_\alpha\tau_s^\alpha, \\
		\bar p_E
		 & \equiv-\lambda
		=\frac{1}{\phi_s}\sum_\alpha\tau_s^\alpha
		=\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha\bar p_s^\alpha .
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
Thus the equivalent pressure is the component-volume-fraction average of the
component pressures; it is not the pressure used to perform the component
Legendre transforms.  The component nonlinear Biot coefficient is the
pressure-held intrinsic-volume sensitivity with respect to the skeleton Jacobian,
%
\begin{equation}
	B_s^\alpha
	=1-\frac{1}{\bar v_{s0}^\alpha}
	\left.\p{\bar v_s^\alpha}{J_s}\right\vert_{\bar p_s^\alpha,\theta,\eta_s^\beta}.
	\label{eq:component_nonlinear_biot_coefficient}
\end{equation}
%
The transformed component stress split is
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^{\prime\alpha}
	=\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^{\prime\prime\alpha}
	+\left(1-B_s^\alpha\right)\bar p_s^\alpha\mbf I .
	\label{eq:component_biot_stress_split}
\end{equation}
%
Substitution into the Coleman--Noll mixture rule gives the solid partial stress
split
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:solid_biot_mixture_rule}
	\begin{align}
		\bs\sigma_s'
		 & =\phi_s\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha
		\bar{\bs\sigma}_s^{\prime\prime\alpha}
		+\phi_s\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha
		\left(1-B_s^\alpha\right)\bar p_s^\alpha\mbf I, \\
		\bs\sigma_s'
		 & \equiv \bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime}
		+\left(1-B_s\right)\bar p_E\mbf I, \\
		1-B_s
		 & =
		\frac{\phi_s}{\bar p_E}
		\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha
		\left(1-B_s^\alpha\right)\bar p_s^\alpha .
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
Thus the solid mixture Biot coefficient is obtained only after the component
pressure contributions have been summed.  If the component pressures equilibrate
to the same value, $\bar p_s^\alpha=\bar p_E$, this reduces to the simpler
volume-fraction average
$1-B_s=\phi_s\sum_\alpha\phi_s^\alpha(1-B_s^\alpha)$.  The porosity factor
enters because $\bs\sigma_s'$ is the partial solid stress, while the component
stresses are intrinsic stresses within the solid phase.

Combining this equivalent-pressure form with
\eqref{eq:solid_biot_mixture_rule} gives
%
\begin{align*}
	\sum_\xi\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	= & \; \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left[
	\bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime}
	+\left(1-B_s\right)
	\bar p_E\mbf I
	\right]
	+\rho\mbf g
	-\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_E
	\\
	 & +\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha
	\dot c_\xi^\alpha\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
\end{align*}
%
Collecting the equivalent-pressure term into the total stress,
%
\begin{align}
	\sum_\xi\rho_\xi\mbf a_\xi
	= & \; \nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left[
	\bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime}-B_s\bar p_E\mbf I
	\right]
	+\rho\mbf g
	\notag \\
	 & +\sum_\alpha\sum_\xi
	\dot c_\xi^\alpha
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
	\label{eq:overall_momentum_nonlinear_biot}
\end{align}
%
When inertia and conversion-momentum production are neglected, this is the
quasistatic total-stress balance used by Kim, Tchelepi, and Juanes, with
$\bar p_E$ replacing the saturation-weighted average pressure.

\subsection{Distension stress and crystallization pressure in practice}
\label{sec:distension_stress_crystallization}
\label{sec:constitutive_crystallization_distension}

The nonlinear Biot transformation above is useful for separating the equivalent
pore-pressure part of the solid stress, but the crystallization response is used
in a different way.  In this formulation, crystallization pressure is not a
primary unknown, not an additional pressure inserted into the momentum balance,
and not a separate Helmholtz-energy postulate.  The primitive constitutive input
is the Helmholtz free energy of the solid constituents together with an
admissible stress update.  Crystallization changes the solid composition, the
source rates, and the distension state; those changes enter the momentum balance
through the stress only after the constitutive stress has been made compatible
with the Helmholtz derivative conjugate to distension.

The practical calculation is therefore:
choose the solid Helmholtz energies, construct a stress update that satisfies
the Coleman--Noll restrictions, solve the balance laws and conversion
constraints for the state, the multipliers, and the mechanism rates, enforce an
admissible kinetic law for those rates, and then report a crystallization
pressure only when it is useful for comparison with classical models or for
using a kinetic law calibrated in terms of supersaturation.  The Coleman--Noll
restriction \eqref{eq:solid_final_distension_pressure} is the compatibility
condition that closes the reversible distension response.  It determines the
hydrostatic part of the stress that is admissibly conjugate to the distension
variable:
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')
	=\rho_s a_s\sum_\alpha\eta_s^\alpha
	\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{a_s}\heldfixed{\mc S}.
	\label{eq:distension_stress_restriction}
\end{equation}
%
Thus any Helmholtz energy dependence on $a_s$ is admissible only with a
corresponding volumetric stress satisfying
\eqref{eq:distension_stress_restriction}.  Conversely, if one wants to prescribe
a distension stress law, the admissible Helmholtz energy must be chosen so that
the same relation is satisfied.  This is the precise sense in which the
distension energy is restricted thermodynamically: the right-hand side is not an
extra source term by itself; it is the required hydrostatic part of the solid
stress.

When the equivalent-pressure split
\eqref{eq:solid_biot_mixture_rule} is used, the same restriction determines the
trace carried by the double-prime solid stress:
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime})
	=
	\rho_s a_s\sum_\alpha\eta_s^\alpha
	\left.\p{\psi_s^\alpha}{a_s}\heldfixed{\mc S}
	-\left(1-B_s\right)\bar p_E .
	\label{eq:double_prime_distension_trace}
\end{equation}
%
With this convention, the total-stress momentum balance
\eqref{eq:overall_momentum_nonlinear_biot} contains the distension contribution
through $\bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime}$ only if the constitutive stress update has
been constrained by \eqref{eq:double_prime_distension_trace}.  The trace relation
is therefore an implementation requirement, not an optional interpretation after
the fact.  Once it has been enforced, no additional crystallization pressure
term should be added to the momentum balance unless a separate non-Helmholtz
mechanism is intentionally introduced.  Crystallization changes the mechanics by
changing the solid composition, source rates, and distension state; the reversible
stress penalty is the Helmholtz force conjugate to $a_s$.

For a simple crystallization-driven distension model, decompose the solid volume
ratio into the distension volume ratio and the true solid volume ratio,
%
\begin{equation}
	J_s=a_s\bar J_s,
	\qquad
	\bar J_s=\frac{J_s}{a_s},
	\label{eq:crystallization_volumetric_split}
\end{equation}
%
where $a_s=\det\mbf A_s$ is the distension volume ratio and $\bar J_s$ is the
true solid volume ratio.  A simple reversible penalty for changing the true
solid volume of component $\alpha$ is
%
\begin{equation}
	W_{\rm mech}^\alpha(J_s,a_s)
	=\frac{K_s^\alpha}{2}
	\left[\ln\left(\frac{J_s}{a_s}\right)\right]^2,
	\label{eq:crystallization_mechanical_energy}
\end{equation}
%
with intrinsic bulk modulus $K_s^\alpha$.  This term vanishes when
$\bar J_s=1$: distension itself is not penalized unless, at the prescribed
skeleton deformation $J_s$, it changes the true solid volume.  In the scalar
specialization represented by \eqref{eq:crystallization_mechanical_energy}, the
Coleman--Noll restriction gives the reversible trace stress
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')
	=-K_s^\alpha\ln\bar J_s .
	\label{eq:crystallization_trace_stress}
\end{equation}
%
This is the specialization of the Coleman--Noll condition
\eqref{eq:CN_distension_force_definition}.  It is a reversible constitutive
restriction produced by the Helmholtz energy, not a kinetic law.

Crystallization enters the rate problem through the mechanism affinity.  The
variational theory has already identified the generalized force conjugate to the
mechanism progress rate $\dot r_m$:
%
\begin{equation}
	\delta W_{\rm conv}
	=-\sum_m\int_{\mc B}\mc A_m\,\delta\dot r_m\,\dv,
	\qquad
	\mc A_m=-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha .
	\label{eq:crystallization_affinity_projection_recall}
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\eqref{eq:crystallization_affinity_projection_recall} is not a second
constitutive assumption.  It is the Euler--Lagrange relation associated with the
conversion constraint, and it projects the solved multipliers onto the
stoichiometric direction of mechanism $m$.  The entropy inequality then requires
the kinetic closure to make this solved driving force admissible:
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_m\dot r_m\ge 0 .
	\label{eq:crystallization_admissible_kinetics}
\end{equation}
%
A simple admissible choice is $\dot r_m=M_m\mc A_m$ with $M_m\ge0$, although a
one-sided precipitation or dissolution law may be used when the mechanism is
not reversible.

This is the point at which a classical crystallization pressure can be recovered.
Consider a mechanism $m$ precipitating solid component $\alpha_m$, and let the
intrinsic specific volume of that solid component be
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}=\frac{1}{\bar\rho_s^{\alpha_m}} .
	\label{eq:crystallization_intrinsic_specific_volume}
\end{equation}
%
After the thermodynamic solve, define the equivalent chemical crystallization
pressure by separating the solved affinity into a chemical pressure scale and
the reversible trace-stress resistance:
%
\begin{equation}
	p_c^{(m)}
	=\frac{\mc A_m}{\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}}
	+\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s') .
	\label{eq:crystallization_affinity_trace_stress}
\end{equation}
%
Equivalently, the affinity driving the kinetic law is
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_m
	=\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}
	\left(
	p_c^{(m)}
	-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')
	\right).
	\label{eq:crystallization_affinity_volumetric}
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\eqref{eq:crystallization_affinity_volumetric} is the operational
meaning of crystallization pressure in the present theory: if a constitutive or
experimental law is written in terms of $p_c^{(m)}$, it must be converted into
the affinity $\mc A_m$ before it is used in the entropy inequality.  The pressure
$p_c^{(m)}$ is therefore a diagnostic pressure scale for the mechanism, while
the actual mechanical stress is the trace stress carried by the admissible
constitutive update for $\bs\sigma_s^{\prime\prime}$.  Using
\eqref{eq:crystallization_trace_stress}, the diagnostic pressure is
%
\begin{equation}
	p_c^{(m)}
	=\frac{\mc A_m}{\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}}
	-K_s^{\alpha_m}\ln\bar J_s .
	\label{eq:crystallization_pressure_from_affinity}
\end{equation}
%
At reversible equilibrium, $\mc A_m=0$, and the chemical pressure balances the
mechanical trace stress:
%
\begin{equation}
	p_c^{(m)}+K_s^{\alpha_m}\ln\bar J_s=0 .
	\label{eq:distension_equilibrium_crystallization}
\end{equation}
%
The usual supersaturation formula can then be read as a diagnostic
reparametrization of this derived pressure:
%
\begin{equation}
	p_c^{(m)}
	=\frac{R\theta}{V_s^{\alpha_m}}\ln\Omega_m,
	\label{eq:classical_crystallization_pressure}
\end{equation}
%
where $V_s^{\alpha_m}$ is the molar volume of the precipitating solid component.
Thus the saturation ratio associated with the solved affinity is
%
\begin{equation}
	\Omega_m
	=\exp\left(
	\frac{V_s^{\alpha_m}}{R\theta}
	\left[
	\frac{\mc A_m}{\bar v_s^{\alpha_m}}
	+\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}(\bs\sigma_s')
	\right]
	\right).
	\label{eq:crystallization_saturation_ratio_diagnostic}
\end{equation}
%
The classical interpretation is recovered after the thermodynamic solve:
$\Omega_m>1$ corresponds to a positive equivalent chemical pressure,
$\Omega_m=1$ to chemical saturation in the absence of mechanical resistance, and
$\Omega_m<1$ to undersaturation.  In this formulation, however, $\Omega_m$ is
not needed as a primary closure variable.  The model is closed by the Helmholtz
free energies, the balance laws, the conversion constraints, and an admissible
kinetic law for $\dot r_m$.  Classical crystallization-pressure formulas may be
used by translating them through
\eqref{eq:crystallization_affinity_volumetric}, but the pressure itself should
not be added a second time to the momentum balance.

\subsection{Model summary, closures, and equation count}
\label{sec:model_summary_closure_count}

This section collects the equations that would be assembled in a simulator.  The
point of the summary is to separate primary unknowns, reversible constitutive
relations generated by the Helmholtz energies, and genuinely dissipative closure
laws.  The component mass balances are
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\mbf v_\xi\right)
	=\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m .
	\label{eq:model_summary_mass_balance}
\end{equation}
%
The phase momentum balances may be kept phasewise in the form
\eqref{eq:el_momentum_uniform}, with the mobile-phase pressure-gradient
specialization shown in
\eqref{eq:el_mom_a_simplified}--\eqref{eq:el_mom_g_simplified}, or summed into
the overall poromechanics form \eqref{eq:overall_momentum_nonlinear_biot}.  The
algebraic volume and composition constraints are
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:model_summary_constraints}
	\begin{align}
		\sum_\xi\phi_\xi
		 & =1, \\
		\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha
		 & =1.
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
with the corresponding Euler--Lagrange relations
%
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:model_summary_pressure_relations}
	\begin{align}
		\lambda+\bar p_l-\gamma_l
		 & =0,
		\qquad l\hbox{ mobile}, \\
		\lambda+\bar p_s
		 & =0, \\
		\sum_\alpha\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi}
		 & =\bar p_\xi .
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
\begin{equation}
	0 =
	\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}+\pi_\xi
	-\rho_\xi
	\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\heldfixed{\mc X_\xi}.
	\label{eq:model_summary_composition_relations}
\end{equation}
%
The solid version of \eqref{eq:model_summary_composition_relations} includes the
component-density chain-rule and stress-free compositional-dilation corrections
in \eqref{eq:CN_solid_composition_multiplier_restriction}.  The intrinsic
pressures, solid stresses, entropy, and distension stress are not independent
laws; they are Coleman--Noll derivatives of the Helmholtz energies summarized by
\eqref{eq:CN_stress_definition}, \eqref{eq:CN_distension_force_definition},
\eqref{eq:CN_phase_pressure_definition}, and \eqref{eq:CN_entropy_definitions}.

The conversion potentials are solved from
\eqref{eq:el_conversion_component}.  After the entropy argument identifies
$L_\xi^\alpha=\mu_\xi^\alpha-\psi_\xi^\alpha$, the mechanism affinity is the
stoichiometric projection
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_m=-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha\mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha .
	\label{eq:model_summary_affinity}
\end{equation}
%
The remaining closure laws are dissipative.  They must be chosen so that
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_m\mc A_m\dot r_m
	+\sum_{\xi<\zeta}\mbf u_{\xi\zeta}\cdot
	\mbf k_{\xi\zeta}^{-1}\mbf u_{\xi\zeta}
	+\sum_\xi
	\frac{\nabla_{\mbf x}\theta\cdot\mbf K_\xi\nabla_{\mbf x}\theta}{\theta}
	\ge 0 .
	\label{eq:model_summary_dissipation}
\end{equation}
%
For example, one may use kinetic laws
$\dot r_m=\mc K_m(\mc A_m,\hbox{state})$ with
$\mc A_m\dot r_m\ge0$, pairwise interaction laws
\eqref{eq:CN_interphase_exchange_law}, and the heat-flux closure
\eqref{eq:CN_heat_flux_closure}.  A crystallization-pressure law, if used, enters
only by translating it into the affinity through
\eqref{eq:crystallization_affinity_volumetric}.

Let $P$ be the number of phases, $N$ the number of components tracked in each
phase, $N_m$ the number of independent conversion mechanisms, and $d$ the spatial
dimension.  A convenient fully implicit pointwise unknown set is
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc Z=
	\left\{
	\phi_\xi,\bar\rho_\xi,\eta_\xi^\alpha,\mbf v_\xi,
	\lambda,\tau_\xi^\alpha,\pi_\xi,\mu_\xi^\alpha,\dot r_m,\theta
	\right\}.
	\label{eq:model_summary_unknown_set}
\end{equation}
%
The number of scalar unknowns is
%
\begin{equation}
	N_{\rm unk}
	=P+dP+3PN+2P+N_m+2
	=dP+3PN+3P+N_m+2 .
	\label{eq:model_summary_unknown_count}
\end{equation}
%
The same number of scalar equations is supplied by the following blocks:
%
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.62\textwidth}c}
	Equation block & Count \\
	\hline
	Component mass balances \eqref{eq:model_summary_mass_balance} & $PN$ \\
	Phase momentum balances \eqref{eq:el_momentum_uniform} & $dP$ \\
	Saturation constraint in \eqref{eq:model_summary_constraints} & $1$ \\
	Volume-fraction Euler--Lagrange relations in \eqref{eq:model_summary_pressure_relations} & $P$ \\
	Intrinsic-density relations in \eqref{eq:model_summary_pressure_relations} & $P$ \\
	Composition stationarity \eqref{eq:model_summary_composition_relations} & $PN$ \\
	Composition normalizations in \eqref{eq:model_summary_constraints} & $P$ \\
	Conversion-potential equations \eqref{eq:el_conversion_component} & $PN$ \\
	Kinetic laws for $\dot r_m$ & $N_m$ \\
	Energy or temperature equation using \eqref{eq:CN_energy_balance} & $1$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
%
Thus
%
\begin{equation}
	N_{\rm eq}
	=PN+dP+1+P+P+PN+P+PN+N_m+1
	=dP+3PN+3P+N_m+2
	=N_{\rm unk}.
	\label{eq:model_summary_equation_count}
\end{equation}
%
This count treats $\mc A_m$ and $\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ as eliminated quantities:
$\mc A_m$ is substituted from \eqref{eq:model_summary_affinity}, and
$\dot c_\xi^\alpha$ is substituted from the stoichiometric source term in
\eqref{eq:model_summary_mass_balance}.  If either is retained as an auxiliary
unknown, the same number of defining equations is added, so the count is
unchanged.  For an isothermal model, remove $\theta$ and the energy equation from
both sides of the count.

\subsection{Relative flux vectors}
\label{sec:relative_flux_vectors}

The phase momentum equations above determine consistent Darcy-scale relative
fluxes once the interphase drag is written in terms of a velocity relative to the
solid skeleton.  For each mobile phase $l$, define the current relative mass
flux by
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf w_l
	=\phi_l\bar\rho_l\left(\mbf v_l-\mbf v_s\right)
	=\rho_l\left(\mbf v_l-\mbf v_s\right).
	\label{eq:relative_mass_flux_definition}
\end{equation}
%
Thus
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf v_l
	=\mbf v_s+\frac{\mbf w_l}{\rho_l}.
	\label{eq:relative_flux_velocity_reconstruction}
\end{equation}
%
The same linear-drag argument used in the reacting-mixture formulation closes
the part of the mobile-phase interaction force produced by motion through the
skeleton as
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf b_l
	=-\frac{\mu_l\phi_l}{\bar\rho_l}\,
	\bs\kappa_l^{-1}\mbf w_l ,
	\label{eq:mobile_phase_drag_law}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mu_l$ is the dynamic viscosity and $\bs\kappa_l$ is the permeability
tensor for phase $l$.  Phase relative permeability may be absorbed into
$\bs\kappa_l$.

Substituting \eqref{eq:relative_flux_velocity_reconstruction} and
\eqref{eq:mobile_phase_drag_law} into the mobile momentum balance
\eqref{eq:el_mom_a_simplified}--\eqref{eq:el_mom_g_simplified} gives, for each
mobile phase,
%
\begin{align}
	\frac{1}{\rho_l}
	\left(
	\phi_l^2\mu_l\bs\kappa_l^{-1}
	+c_l\mbf I
	\right)\mbf w_l
	= & \;
	\rho_l\left(\mbf g-\mbf a_l\right)
	-\phi_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l
	\notag \\
	& +\sum_\alpha\dot c_l^\alpha
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_l^\alpha-\mbf v_s\right),
	\label{eq:relative_flux_linear_system}
\end{align}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
	c_l=\sum_\alpha\dot c_l^\alpha .
	\label{eq:mobile_total_source_rate}
\end{equation}
%
The term $c_l\mbf I$ is the same reaction-induced correction that appears in
the three-phase reacting-mixture flux law.  It comes from the velocity carried
inside the conversion-momentum term, not from appending a source to the mass
balance after the fact.

Define the modified permeability tensor
%
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\bs\kappa}_l
	=
	\left(
	\phi_l^2\mu_l\bs\kappa_l^{-1}
	+c_l\mbf I
	\right)^{-1}.
	\label{eq:modified_relative_flux_permeability}
\end{equation}
%
Then the admissible relative mass flux is
%
\begin{align}
	\mbf w_l
	= \rho_l\widetilde{\bs\kappa}_l
	\bigg[
	\rho_l\left(\mbf g-\mbf a_l\right)
	-\phi_l\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l
	+\sum_\alpha\dot c_l^\alpha
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_l^\alpha-\mbf v_s\right)
	\bigg].
	\label{eq:generalized_mobile_darcy_mass_flux}
\end{align}
%
This expression is the multicomponent mobile-phase analogue of the generalized
Darcy flux in the earlier reacting-mixture model.  The first two terms are the
usual body-force and pressure-gradient contributions.  The last term is the
thermodynamic conversion correction: mass transfer into or out of phase $l$
drives relative motion through gradients of the solved component potentials
$\mu_l^\alpha$ and through the momentum carried by the solid frame.

When conversion is absent, $c_l=0$ and $\dot c_l^\alpha=0$, and when mobile-phase
inertia is negligible, \eqref{eq:generalized_mobile_darcy_mass_flux} reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf w_l
	=\bar\rho_l\frac{\bs\kappa_l}{\mu_l}
	\left(\bar\rho_l\mbf g-\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l\right).
	\label{eq:standard_darcy_mass_flux_limit}
\end{equation}
%
Equivalently, for the relative volumetric flux
$\mbf q_l=\phi_l(\mbf v_l-\mbf v_s)=\mbf w_l/\bar\rho_l$,
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf q_l
	=-\frac{\bs\kappa_l}{\mu_l}
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_l-\bar\rho_l\mbf g\right),
	\label{eq:standard_darcy_volumetric_flux_limit}
\end{equation}
%
which is Darcy's law for phase $l$ relative to the deforming solid skeleton.  In
the reacting case, \eqref{eq:generalized_mobile_darcy_mass_flux} should be used
instead of \eqref{eq:standard_darcy_volumetric_flux_limit} whenever conversion
rates are large enough for momentum production or gradients of
$\mu_l^\alpha$ to affect transport.

\subsection{Classical compositional-flow limit and interpretation of the multipliers}
\label{sec:classical_compositional_limit}

The preceding equations contain the usual compositional-reservoir structure as a
special limit.  The phase/component source is written as
%
\begin{equation}
	\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m,
	\label{eq:classical_source_form}
\end{equation}
%
which is the phase-resolved analogue of the standard reactive-transport source
vector $\mbf R=\bs\nu\mbf q$.  Summing \eqref{eq:spatial_mass_2} over phases gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot\left(\sum_\xi\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha\mbf v_\xi\right)
	=\sum_\xi\sum_m\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha\dot r_m .
	\label{eq:classical_component_balance}
\end{equation}
%
To see the usual reservoir form, restrict attention to the mobile pore phases and
write their volume fractions as
%
\begin{equation}
	\phi_\xi=\phi S_\xi,
	\qquad
	\sum_{\xi\in\{a,o,g\}}S_\xi=1,
	\label{eq:classical_saturation_identification}
\end{equation}
%
where $\phi$ is porosity and $S_\xi$ is the saturation of phase $\xi$.  Let
$M^\alpha$ be the molar mass of component $\alpha$, and define the phase
component molar concentration per phase volume by
%
\begin{equation}
	\bar C_\xi^\alpha
	=\frac{\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha}{M^\alpha}.
	\label{eq:classical_molar_concentration}
\end{equation}
%
Dividing \eqref{eq:spatial_mass_2} by $M^\alpha$ gives the phase-resolved molar
balance
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi S_\xi\bar C_\xi^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\phi S_\xi\bar C_\xi^\alpha\mbf v_\xi\right)
	=\sum_m\frac{\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha}{M^\alpha}\dot r_m .
	\label{eq:classical_phase_molar_balance}
\end{equation}
%
If the porous skeleton is fixed, or if the equation is written relative to the
skeleton and the advective solid term is moved to the left-hand storage operator,
the phase Darcy flux is
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf q_\xi=\phi S_\xi(\mbf v_\xi-\mbf v_s).
	\label{eq:classical_darcy_flux_definition}
\end{equation}
%
For the fixed-skeleton case $\mbf v_s=\mbf 0$, so
\eqref{eq:classical_phase_molar_balance} becomes
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi S_\xi\bar C_\xi^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\bar C_\xi^\alpha\mbf q_\xi\right)
	=\sum_m\frac{\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha}{M^\alpha}\dot r_m .
	\label{eq:classical_phase_molar_darcy_balance}
\end{equation}
%
This is the phase/component conservation equation used before the phase equations
are summed in a compositional simulator.  A standard Darcy closure supplies
%
\begin{equation}
	\mbf q_\xi
	=-\frac{k k_{r\xi}}{\mu_\xi}
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\bar p_\xi-\bar\rho_\xi\mbf g\right),
	\label{eq:classical_darcy_closure}
\end{equation}
%
possibly with capillary-pressure relations between the phase pressures.  In the
present theory this is not postulated at the balance-law level: it is a closure
that replaces the mobile-phase momentum equations
\eqref{eq:el_mom_a_simplified}--\eqref{eq:el_mom_g_simplified} after inertia,
body interactions, and conversion-momentum terms have been specialized.

Summing \eqref{eq:classical_phase_molar_darcy_balance} over mobile phases yields
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
	\left(\phi\sum_\xi S_\xi\bar C_\xi^\alpha\right)
	+\nabla_{\mbf x}\cdot
	\left(\sum_\xi\bar C_\xi^\alpha\mbf q_\xi\right)
	=\sum_\xi\sum_m\frac{\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha}{M^\alpha}\dot r_m .
	\label{eq:classical_total_molar_component_balance}
\end{equation}
%
This is the conservative component balance used in compositional reservoir
simulation.  The usual simulator source for component $\alpha$ is therefore the
phase-summed stoichiometric projection on the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:classical_total_molar_component_balance}.  The difference is that the
present coefficients retain the phase label.  That phase label permits a single
mechanism to encode both chemical conversion and the phase in which the product
appears.  A pure phase transformation is the special case in which the component
label is unchanged and the two nonzero stoichiometric entries are equal and
opposite; after summing over phases, its contribution to the total component
source vanishes, but it still changes the phase saturations and phase
compositions.

The remaining algebraic structure of a traditional compositional simulator is
also recovered as a closure of the present variables.  The saturation constraint
\eqref{eq:classical_saturation_identification} is the mobile-phase version of
$\sum_\xi\phi_\xi=1$ after the solid volume fraction has been separated.  The
within-phase composition constraints are
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1
	\qquad\hbox{or, equivalently,}\qquad
	x_\xi^\alpha
	=\frac{\bar C_\xi^\alpha}{\sum_\beta\bar C_\xi^\beta},
	\quad
	\sum_\alpha x_\xi^\alpha=1 ,
	\label{eq:classical_composition_constraints}
\end{equation}
%
This comparison highlights the central thermodynamic distinction.  A traditional
compositional simulator typically supplies an equation of state, phase
equilibrium rules, capillary-pressure relations, component partitioning, and
mechanical stress laws as separate closure ingredients.  In the present
formulation the reversible thermodynamic part is instead generated from the
Helmholtz free-energy structure:
%
\begin{equation}
	\psi_\xi
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	\bar p_\xi,\quad
	s_\xi,\quad
	\bs\sigma_s',\quad
	\rho_\xi\left.\p{\psi_\xi}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}\right\vert_{\cdots},\quad
	\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\eta_\xi^\alpha}.
	\label{eq:helmholtz_generates_reversible_closures}
\end{equation}
%
Thus pressure, entropy, stress, composition derivatives, and normalized
component storage potentials are not independent reversible closures; they are
conjugate derivatives or multipliers associated with the constrained Helmholtz
structure.  Transport and reaction kinetics still require dissipative closures,
such as mobilities, permeabilities, diffusivities, and reaction-rate laws, but
the thermodynamic forces driving those laws are generated by the same
free-energy potential.

where $x_\xi^\alpha$ is the phase mole fraction.  Flash calculations then impose
phase-equilibrium closures, commonly written as fugacity equalities.  In the
present notation the reversible version of such a phase transfer for component
$\alpha$ from phase $\xi$ to phase $\zeta$ has
$\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=-1$ and $\nu_{\zeta m}^\alpha=+1$, so
\eqref{eq:classical_equilibrium_affinity} reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
	\mu_\zeta^\alpha-\mu_\xi^\alpha=0,
	\label{eq:classical_phase_equilibrium_mu}
\end{equation}
%
which is the chemical-potential form of the fugacity-equality condition used in
isothermal phase equilibrium.

The multiplier $\mu_\xi^\alpha$ in \eqref{eq:C4_def} is the chemical-potential-like
variable conjugate to phase/component conversion.  Its evolution equation is
%
\begin{equation}
	\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
	=\frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
	-\psi_\xi
	-L_\xi^\alpha
	-\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha} .
	\label{eq:mu_interpretation}
\end{equation}
%
The first term is the kinetic contribution associated with adding mass to a
phase moving with velocity $\mbf v_\xi$.  The next two terms are the Helmholtz
free energy and component conversion-work contribution.  The last term is the
contribution of the component mass constraint; $\tau_\xi^\alpha$ is the
multiplier enforcing the storage relation between the reference amount of
component $\alpha$ and the current partial mass density
$\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha$.  After the entropy inequality identifies
$L_\xi^\alpha=\mu_\xi^\alpha-\psi_\xi^\alpha$, this equation becomes
\begin{equation}
	\frac{D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha}{Dt}
	=\frac{1}{2}\mbf v_\xi\cdot\mbf v_\xi
	-\mu_\xi^\alpha
	-\frac{\tau_\xi^\alpha}{\phi_\xi\bar\rho_\xi\eta_\xi^\alpha} .
	\label{eq:mu_interpretation_entropy_closure}
\end{equation}
Thus
$D_\xi\mu_\xi^\alpha/Dt$ is a generalized chemical-mechanical potential.  In a
quasi-static isothermal limit with negligible kinetic terms and a conventional
choice of thermodynamic potential, this quantity reduces to the chemical
potential (or, equivalently in reservoir practice, to a fugacity/equilibrium
potential) that appears in phase and reaction equilibrium conditions.

The affinity is the stoichiometric projection of these phase/component
potentials:
%
\begin{equation}
	\mc A_m
	=-\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha .
	\label{eq:classical_affinity_projection}
\end{equation}
%
For an equilibrated mechanism, imposing $\mc A_m=0$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\xi\sum_\alpha \mu_\xi^\alpha\nu_{\xi m}^\alpha=0,
	\label{eq:classical_equilibrium_affinity}
\end{equation}
%
which is the classical statement that the stoichiometric sum of chemical
potentials vanishes.  For a kinetically limited mechanism, such as a slow
fluid--solid reaction, \eqref{eq:classical_affinity_projection} instead supplies
the driving force in a closure
%
\begin{equation}
	\dot r_m=\mc K_m(\mc A_m,\text{state}),
	\qquad \mc A_m\dot r_m\ge 0
\end{equation}
%
with the sign convention used here.  Thus equilibrium chemistry, kinetic
reactive transport, and phase transformation all use the same stoichiometric
projection; only the closure for $\dot r_m$ changes.

The momentum equations contain an additional conversion term absent from purely
kinematic Darcy-scale compositional balances,
%
\begin{equation}
	\sum_\alpha\sum_\xi\dot c_\xi^\alpha
	\left(\nabla_{\mbf x}\mu_\xi^\alpha-\mbf v_\xi\right).
	\label{eq:conversion_momentum_interpretation}
\end{equation}
%
The term combines the inertial bookkeeping required when mass is removed from or
added to a moving phase with the gradient force generated by variation of the
current-rate constraint $\dot c_\xi^\alpha=\dot{\mc{C}}_\xi^\alpha/J_\xi$.  Total
mass conservation constrains the component source rates, but the explicit form
above avoids introducing an additional conversion-velocity notation.  These terms
vanish or are hidden in classical reservoir models when phase momenta are not
solved explicitly and the phase velocities are supplied by Darcy laws.

Finally, the remaining multipliers have standard constraint interpretations:
$\lambda$ enforces saturation of the pore volume by the phases,
$\pi_\xi$ enforces $\sum_\alpha\eta_\xi^\alpha=1$, and $\tau_\xi^\alpha$ enforces
the phase/component storage relation.  The novelty of the present formulation is
not the stoichiometric source form itself, but the fact that it is embedded in a
phase-attached variational mechanics in which the Jacobian $J_\xi$ is varied.
That is what exposes the conversion-induced momentum production terms and gives
a direct bridge between mixture mechanics and classical compositional reactive
transport.
